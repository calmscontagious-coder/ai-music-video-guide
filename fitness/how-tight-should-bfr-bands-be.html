How Tight Should BFR Bands Be? Pressure, Not Feel (2026)

By Purpose with Presence FitnessUpdated November 6, 202610 min read

Quick answer: How tight should BFR bands be? Set them to a percentage of arterial occlusion pressure, 40 to 80%, measured on your own limb with the cuff you will use, using Doppler ultrasound or a device built to measure it. Feel is unreliable, and a copied number may sit at or above your own occlusion pressure.

This page answers one narrow question from our blood flow restriction training beginner guide: how tight a cuff or band should be. The short version is that tightness is the wrong thing to aim for. The target is a pressure, expressed relative to the pressure that stops blood flow in your own limb, and it has to be measured, not felt.

We are a site team, not clinicians, and we have not tested any cuff. For the wider comparison of programs, see our fitness program comparison.

What does the research say about setting BFR pressure?

The 2019 review by Patterson and colleagues states that it is recommended to set pressure based on a measurement of arterial occlusion pressure, called AOP, and that pressures from 40 to 80% of AOP have evidence to support their efficacy. AOP is the pressure at which blood flow into the limb stops. So the instruction is not "make it tight". It is "find the pressure that fully stops arterial flow in this limb, with this cuff, then use a stated fraction of it".

The same review describes the method as partially restricting arterial inflow and fully restricting venous outflow. A percentage of AOP is how that partial restriction is dialled in. Without the AOP measurement, a percentage has nothing to be a percentage of.

Can you judge BFR tightness by feel?

No, and a study tested it directly. In Bell and colleagues (2020), 24 participants (12 men and 12 women) were tested over 3 days using a perceived tightness scale, where people rate how tight the cuff feels. The percentage coefficient of variation for the measurement was approximately 12%, which the authors say would produce an overestimation or underestimation of about 25% from the mean perceived pressure in the upper body and about 20% in the lower body.

The direction of the error also differed by limb. Participants rated pressures above their AOP for the upper body and below for the lower body. The authors concluded that the scale does not provide reliable estimates of relative pressures over multiple visits.

In practice this means a participant's own rating of seven out of ten on the scale landed at widely different fractions of their AOP from day to day, and on average above AOP for the arm and below it for the leg. A rating copied from a video or a friend was not tested. A rating describes a sensation. It does not describe how much blood is still getting through.

Why does the cuff change the number?

Because the pressure needed to stop blood flow depends on how wide the cuff is. The 2019 review says AOP is largely determined by the width of the cuff, with a wider cuff requiring a lower pressure. It lists cuff sizes of 5 (small), 10 or 12 (medium), and 17 or 18 cm (large).

The size of the difference is large. In Loenneke and colleagues (2012), 116 participants were tested supine with a 5 cm narrow cuff and a 13.5 cm wide cuff on the upper thigh. Arterial occlusion differed between cuff types: narrow 235 (42) mmHg versus wide 144 (17) mmHg. These are resting thigh values for those two cuffs, not settings. The authors suggested that future studies account for the width of the cuff used.

A larger arm dataset shows the same pattern with three widths. In Jessee and colleagues (2016), 249 participants were tested. The 5-cm-wide cuff required the greatest inflation pressure, 145 (19) mmHg, followed by the 10 cm at 123 (13) mmHg, and the 12-cm-wide cuff at 120 (12) mmHg.

Does cuff material matter?

Material, or something that travels with it, can change the reading. In Buckner and colleagues (2017), a study of the upper arm with the person standing, resting arterial occlusion pressure was 139 (14) mmHg with a 5 cm nylon cuff and 246 (71) mmHg with a 3 cm elastic cuff. When each cuff was inflated to 40% of its own occlusion pressure, there were no differences in repetitions to failure, torque, muscle thickness, or muscle activation between them.

What predicts your own occlusion pressure?

Body size and blood pressure. A scoping review by Trybulski and colleagues (2026) included 17 studies and found that limb circumference and resting blood pressure were the most commonly examined factors and showed the most consistent associations with AOP. Evidence for sex and limb composition was more variable, and the authors note that substantial methodological heterogeneity makes comparisons across studies difficult.

The arm data agree. In the Jessee 2016 analysis, arm circumference explained the most unique variance for each cuff, and the prediction models for all three cuffs included baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, arm length, and sex. After controlling for sex, non-Hispanic Blacks required greater pressures than Whites, though the authors say it is not known how meaningful this is.

For the thigh, Loenneke 2012 concluded that restrictive cuff pressures should be largely based on thigh circumference and not on pressures previously used in the literature. In short, two people with different limb sizes or blood pressure should not expect the same setting, even on the same cuff. These are population patterns, not a formula you should apply to yourself by hand.

How is occlusion pressure actually measured?

With an ultrasound Doppler on the artery while the cuff is inflated, finding the pressure at which the pulse signal disappears. Vehrs and colleagues (2025) made simultaneous measures of brachial artery AOP in 20 males and 21 females using pulse wave and continuous wave Doppler devices. The overall difference was not significant: 121.7 ± 14.7 mmHg with pulse wave and 121.3 ± 14.5 mmHg with continuous wave. The authors state that a hand-held continuous wave Doppler device and a handpump with a gauge can be used to measure AOP.

That result is about two measurement devices agreeing on the upper arm in one lab sample. It does not mean a home reading is easy to do well. Placing the probe, reading the signal and inflating in a controlled way take practice. Who should do the measurement is our editorial caution, not a finding of these studies. Some cuffs on the market measure occlusion pressure for you; our band comparison lists the ones we found and what their makers say.

If you want to see how a percentage translates into a pressure once an AOP has been measured, the BFR cuff pressure calculator does the multiplication. It does not measure anything, so it cannot replace the measurement.

Why is a number from someone else not a safe setting?

Because the same absolute pressure can be well below one person's AOP and above another's. Loenneke and colleagues (2013) described, in an opinion article, a lab dataset of 83 participants measured supine with a 5 cm cuff: 19 of them would be at or above their arterial occlusion pressure at an arbitrary BFR pressure of 200 mmHg. They wrote that pressures should be relative to the individual, just as the loads lifted in those studies were relative to the individual, and that their recommendations apply only to the lower body.

What about studies that used wraps and no measured pressure?

Some do exist, and they describe their own method rather than offering a rule. In Wilson and colleagues (2013), 12 trained male participants did leg press under control and low-intensity BFR conditions. The study states that knee wraps were applied to the thighs at a pressure that resulted in venous, not arterial, occlusion, and that in the control trial wraps were applied with zero pressure. In that single acute session, the 12 trained men doing leg press at 30% 1RM showed greater muscle activation and muscle thickness with the wraps and no increase in the damage measures tracked (soreness, power, swelling). The study did not test safety in other people, over repeated sessions, or with any pressure we could name, and we are not offering it as a method.

What if you feel numbness during or after a session?

Our precaution, not a source instruction: if a limb goes numb during or after a session, stop and ask a clinician. The 2019 review reports that a wide range of pressures applied by practitioners resulted in unintended consequences, such as a large incidence of numbness following BFR. In other words, numbness is one of the things that tends to show up when pressure is not set carefully.

A survey of practitioners gives a sense of how often it is reported. In Patterson and Brandner (2018), data were obtained from 250 participants, with 115 stating that they had prescribed BFR as an intervention. The incidence rate of side effects was largest for delayed onset muscle soreness (39.2%), numbness (18.5%), fainting/dizziness (14.6%) and bruising (13.1%). Those are reported incidences from a questionnaire, not a safety threshold, and they do not tell you what any one person should do. We give no symptom rules beyond our own precaution above, because the sources we read do not give them.

What should you do before you set a pressure?

Four steps, in order.

  • Check whether you should be cleared first. Read our page on BFR training contraindications and speak to a qualified professional before starting.
  • Get an AOP measured on the limb you will train, with the cuff you will use, ideally with a clinician or practitioner who has done it before (our advice, not a source requirement).
  • Use a stated fraction of that AOP, inside the 40 to 80% range the 2019 review describes, and write down the cuff, the limb and the percentage.
  • Treat a measurement as valid only for the cuff, limb and body position it was taken in: the studies show that cuff width, limb size, resting blood pressure and body position all change the number. No source states how often to re-measure.

If you cannot get an AOP measured, we do not have a source that supports a do-it-yourself substitute, so we are not offering one. Our guide to getting started with BFR sets out the rest of the method, and the fitness hub lists the other guides.

Frequently asked questions

What percentage of arterial occlusion pressure should BFR bands use?+

The 2019 review says pressures from 40 to 80% of arterial occlusion pressure have evidence to support their efficacy. It does not say which point in that range suits a given person, and it is an efficacy range, not a safety guarantee. The percentage only works if arterial occlusion pressure was measured on your limb with your cuff.

Can I set BFR band tightness by feel?+

Not reliably. A study of 24 participants found the perceived tightness scale did not give reliable estimates of relative pressure across visits, with errors of about 25% in the upper body and about 20% in the lower body. People rated pressures above their occlusion pressure for the arm and below it for the leg.

Why do wide and narrow cuffs need different pressures?+

A wider cuff stops arterial flow at a lower pressure. In one study of 116 people lying supine, a 5 cm cuff on the thigh needed 235 (42) mmHg and a 13.5 cm cuff needed 144 (17) mmHg. These are lab measurements, not pressures to use, and a pressure from a different width or material does not transfer to your cuff.

Is the pressure on a pump gauge the same as the pressure I should use?+

Only if it was chosen relative to your own occlusion pressure. The same gauge reading can sit below one person's occlusion pressure and above another's, depending on limb size, blood pressure, and cuff width, so a number from a video or a friend tells you little about your own limb.

What should I do if my limb goes numb during BFR?+

Our precaution, not a source instruction: stop and ask a clinician. The 2019 review reports numbness as an unintended consequence of the wide range of pressures used in practice. We give no other symptom rules because the sources we read do not set any. Mention the cuff, the pressure and how long it lasted.
