What does a free plan already give a beginner?

More than most people expect. The NHS guidelines for adults aged 19 to 64 ask for strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups (legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders and arms) on at least 2 days a week. The same page says to work to the point where you need a short rest before repeating the activity, and to speak to your GP first if you have not exercised for some time or have medical conditions or concerns.

The NHS strength exercises page is a set of gentle chair and wall exercises for people who have not done much recently. It says to do them at least twice a week and to build up slowly, increasing repetitions gradually; its wall push-up is given as 3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions. Together with the guidelines page, that is a free starting frequency from a national health service.

The American College of Sports Medicine (2009) position stand on progression models adds the training-science layer for novices. It recommends loads that correspond to an 8-12 repetition maximum (RM), and a training frequency of 2-3 days a week for novices. For progression it says a 2-10% increase in load should be applied when you can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number. It also notes that its recommendations should be applied in context and depend on a person's goals, physical capacity and training status.

Does adding sets matter, and is a plan the only way to get them?

Krieger (2010) pooled 8 studies and found that multiple sets were associated with a larger effect size than a single set, with a tendency for effect sizes to rise from 0.24 for 1 set to 0.34 for 2-3 sets and 0.44 for 4-6 sets. Krieger's pooled result supports doing more than one set per exercise; the 2-3 set range is a trend in his dose-response model (p = 0.09), not a settled figure. It does not say you need a paid product to run them.

What does a free week look like in practice?

We built one so you can judge for yourself. Our free two-day dumbbell-only sample week pairs a goblet squat with a dumbbell Romanian deadlift, a dumbbell bench press (or floor press) with a one-arm row, and, if time allows, a split squat with a single-leg Romanian deadlift. It uses 2-3 sets per exercise and 1 to 2 min rest. It costs nothing, and the page explains the research behind each range.

What the free route asks of you is the work around the list: choosing a weight, recording what you lifted, deciding when to add load, and swapping an exercise when one hurts or a piece of equipment is missing. The ACSM rule above makes the progression decision mechanical: when you can do one to two repetitions more than the target, raise the load by 2-10%. Someone willing to keep a notebook can run that rule alone.

Two honest limits apply. A free plan does not show you the movement: a written line saying "goblet squat" does not tell you where your knees should point. Free video exists and its quality varies, so you have to find it and judge it. And a plan built from ranges gives you the same list each week unless you rewrite it, which is a planning job that some people drop after a month.

Who should stay on a free plan, and who might pay?

Stay on a free plan if

  • You are comfortable keeping a short training log and applying the 2-10% progression rule yourself.
  • You already know the basic lifts, or you are happy to learn them from free, reputable sources.
  • You are still testing whether you will train two days a week at all. The NHS number is at least 2 days; prove that habit first.
  • You want to keep the cost at zero while you check how your body responds.

Consider paying if

  • You want a fixed plan where each week is already written, so there is nothing to decide at the gym.
  • You learn better from video demonstrations than from written lists.
  • You will not plan your own progression, and a program that tells you what comes next is the difference between training and not training.
  • You have a bench and dumbbells and want the equipment question settled before you start.

Neither group is wrong. In a free workout plan vs paid program choice, the decision is about how you work, not about which route produces more muscle.
