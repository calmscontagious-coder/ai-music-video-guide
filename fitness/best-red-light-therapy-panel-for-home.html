What do red and near-infrared light therapy panels actually do?

Photobiomodulation means shining red or near-infrared light on tissue. Hamblin (2019) notes that early studies mainly used red light (600 to 700 nm), and that near-infrared light (760 to 1000+ nm) was later found to be equally, if not more, effective. Shi and colleagues (2026) describe one multilayer skin model in which the fluence rate fell to 1% of the incident value at about 1.6 mm for 450-nm light and at approximately 4.8 to 5 mm for 650-nm light. The authors call these values model-dependent rather than universal tissue constants.

What does the research support, and what does it not, by use?

Skin

Wunsch and Matuschka (2014) ran a randomized, controlled study of 136 volunteers. Of these, 113 were assigned to four light groups (611-650 or 570-850 nm light, normalized to about 9 J/cm2 in the 611-650 nm range, twice a week) and compared with controls (n=23). After 30 sessions the light groups showed improved skin complexion, roughness and ultrasonographically measured collagen density, and broadband light showed no advantage over red-only light. The lamps ran at 5.9 to 13.3 mW/cm2 for 12 to 25 minutes, so the study did not test the consumer panels in this guide.

Das and colleagues (2026) reviewed at-home LED devices for facial rejuvenation: eight studies, 365 participants, wavelengths from 440 to 1072 nm. The authors note varied protocols, limited comparative data and underrepresentation of diverse skin phenotypes. For acne, Ershadi and Barbieri (2025) pooled 6 studies and found a 45.3% greater change in inflammatory lesions than control (95% CI, 25.1% to 65.5%), with moderate to high heterogeneity and possible publication bias. The devices were at-home red and/or blue LED devices, not panels; the heterogeneity came from light source, fluence and duration of use.

Muscle recovery and performance

Leal-Junior and colleagues (2015) found 13 acceptable randomized trials. Of those, 12 applied light before exercise, and 10 reported significant performance gains. Time until exhaustion rose by 4.12 s versus placebo. Vanin and colleagues (2018) included 39 trials (861 participants), of which 28 were meta-analysed against placebo, rated the evidence very low to moderate in quality, and cited small samples and great variability in protocols. Zhai and colleagues (2026) pooled 23 trials and found small effects on endurance (p = 0.02) and strength (p = 0.04), and call the subgroup findings exploratory.

The dose language in these reviews is per point, per diode and per application site, which describes devices aimed at specific muscles. They do not test a whole-body panel.

Hair

Perez and colleagues (2025) pooled 38 studies describing 3,098 patients, of whom 2,930 had androgenetic alopecia. Hair density rose versus placebo after low-level laser or LED therapy for 4 to 26 weeks, and the abstract reports an SMD of 1.44 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.91) for the 20-week analysis, not for the whole range, with high heterogeneity (I2 = 80.81%). The devices were mostly lasers and LED products compared before and after therapy with placebo, so the pooled result does not describe panels. Gupta and colleagues (2025) are more guarded: efficacy as a standalone approach remains debated, device parameters were inconsistently reported, and none of the devices have been FDA cleared for Fitzpatrick skin types V and VI.

Pain

Chow and colleagues (2009) pooled 16 randomized laser trials with 820 patients for neck pain: pain intensity fell by 19.86 mm across 11 trials, and side-effects were mild. For knee osteoarthritis, Shi and colleagues (2026) cite a meta-analysis of 10 placebo-controlled trials with 542 participants showing a moderate reduction in pain at rest, with very low certainty.

Why do the irradiance numbers on product pages disagree?

Irradiance (mW/cm2) depends on the meter, the distance and the area measured. A retailer blog that sells panels (not independent) says solar meters, which are designed for sunlight or grow lights, inflate figures, and that spectrometer readings of quality panels typically show 20 to 40 mW/cm2 at 30cm. That is a seller's opinion. The Mito testing page is also a seller's statement: Mito says broadband solar meters overstate irradiance by roughly 2.2x to 2.5x versus an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory, and that its primary values were taken by LightLab International at 6 inches.

The figures in this guide come from different methods and are not directly comparable. The BIOMAX 300 figure is a broadband (350-1700nm) average from a lab report, the Mito figures use an "Area Below LEDs" methodology, and the Hooga figures are one reviewer's spectrometer readings. Do not rank panels by these numbers.

One example of the gap is Hooga. The Light Therapy Insiders review of the Hooga ULTRA1500 reports that the website says greater than 165mW/cm2 at 6 inches while the manual says 100mW/cm2. The reviewer's spectrometer showed a peak of 104mW/cm2 and an average of 81.7mW/cm2 across 9 places. The reviewer states that he earns a commission on purchases through his links, and these are readings from one unit on his own instrument. The Hooga certifications page lists the same 104 peak and 81.7 average readings at 6 inches and says published irradiance figures are manufacturer specifications. For the Hooga PRO1500, the Light Therapy Insiders review of the Hooga PRO1500 recorded a peak of 96mW/cm2 and an average of 86mW/cm2 at six inches.

What does the FDA wording on these panels actually mean?

The American Academy of Dermatology states that the FDA clears devices and does not approve or certify them, and that cleared tells you nothing about how effective a device is. Harvard Health Publishing says a device labeled FDA-cleared means the agency considers it safe, but none of the panels described here is called cleared by its maker.

The category matters most. The Hooga certifications page says its panels are listed as Class II devices, product code ILY (21 CFR 890.5500), "a 510(k)-exempt category," and that "This is the same FDA category Mito Red Light, Joovv and PlatinumLED panels are listed under." That is a heating-lamp category. A listing there says nothing about the skin, hair or recovery studies above, and "Class II" names a device class, not a clearance or proof that a panel works.

Each maker's wording, from the pages we could read:

  • PlatinumLED, PlatinumLED home page: "official FDA Class II Medical Devices and manufactured in our FDA registered facility."
  • Mito, Mito FAQ: "Mito Red Light is registered with the FDA and a certain subset of Mito Red products have obtained 510(k) clearance." The cleared products it names are the MitoGROW Helmet and MitoCLEAR and MitoGLOW Masks; other Mito devices are described as excluded from the 510(k) pathway.
  • Hooga, Hooga certifications page: panels are "listed with the FDA as Class II devices under product code ILY," and "An FDA listing is not FDA approval or clearance."
  • Bestqool, Bestqool home page: "Class II medical devices, 510(k) exempt under 21CFR§ 890.5500."
  • Joovv, Joovv home page, footnote: "*Joovv light therapy products are intended to provide topical heating for the purpose of elevating tissue temperature for Temporary relief of minor muscle pain, Joint pain, Minor arthritis pain, Muscle spasms, Temporary increase in local blood circulation, Temporary relaxation of muscles." The page also shows the Intertek safety certification mark.

Put together: "registered" and "listed" mean the maker or device is on an FDA list, which Hooga says is not clearance. "510(k) clearance" is FDA clearance of that device, which the AAD says tells you nothing about effectiveness. "510(k) exempt" is how Hooga describes its category, and Mito describes its other devices as excluded from the 510(k) pathway; neither describes those panels as cleared. None of the panels described here is called 510(k) cleared by its maker. Mito's cleared products are a helmet and masks. We could not read the Joovv or Bestqool product pages, so we rely on their home pages.

Is red light therapy safe, and what about your eyes?

The American Academy of Dermatology says the most common side effects are mild, that a light-sensitive condition such as lupus can worsen with red light, and that if you take medication that increases light sensitivity, red light may not be recommended. Harvard Health Publishing adds that you should avoid use with light-sensitive medications such as certain antibiotics, and wear eye protection if the directions recommend it.

Labkovich and colleagues (2026) describe a 43-year-old woman who developed acute bilateral angle-closure glaucoma after using a red-light mask for two weeks. The makers do not recommend eye protection and she wore none. This is one case report, so it cannot show cause, but the authors believe eye protection should be considered. Shi and colleagues (2026) note that safety cannot be inferred from wavelength alone and that visible red wavelengths fall within the retinal-hazard spectral region, where ocular focusing can increase retinal irradiance. Cronshaw and colleagues (2025) tested five intra-oral and hand-held LED devices, not panels. Their eyewear attenuated 55 to 88% depending on device and wavelength, which means up to 45% of the light still passes, and they call the eyewear of poor build quality and comparable to inexpensive sunglasses. We found no study of the eyewear sold with panels. Wear eye protection if the directions recommend it, and do not take a pair of glasses as proof of safety.

How do you use a panel without overdoing it?

Few dose and timing facts are solid, and none comes from a whole-body panel protocol; we found none for the panels in this guide. Dose in J/cm2 is irradiance multiplied by time: in the Wunsch and Matuschka (2014) table, 7.1 mW/cm2 for 20 min gave 8.5 J/cm2. That study used 30 sessions. Harvard Health Publishing says scientists have not pinned down how long each session should last.

Distance changes dose: BIOMAX 300 average irradiance fell from 71.79 mW/cm^2 at 6 inches to 56.25 at 12 inches. No published protocol covers a whole-body panel for muscle recovery, so we give no timing. Because of the biphasic response in Huang and colleagues (2009), longer or closer is not a safe assumption. Follow the maker's directions, and see a clinician for eye pain or vision loss.

Frequently asked questions

Does red light therapy help muscle recovery?+

The evidence is mixed and mostly not about panels. Leal-Junior and colleagues (2015) found 12 of 13 acceptable trials applied light before exercise, with 10 reporting performance gains. Zhai and colleagues (2026) found small effects and called subgroup findings exploratory. Most dose terms are per point or per diode, and whole-body panels are not covered by these reviews.

Why do panel irradiance numbers disagree?+

Makers use different meters, distances and areas. Mito, a seller, says broadband solar meters overstate output by roughly 2.2x to 2.5x versus an accredited lab. A reviewer who earns commission measured the Hooga ULTRA1500 at a 104mW/cm2 peak at 6 inches, while the site had claimed more than 165mW/cm2. Compare only figures with distance and method stated.

Is FDA registered the same as FDA cleared?+

No. Hooga states that an FDA listing is not FDA approval or clearance, and that its panels sit in a 510(k)-exempt heating-lamp category. The American Academy of Dermatology says the FDA clears devices and does not approve or certify them, and that cleared tells you nothing about effectiveness. Class II names a device class, not proof a panel works.

Do I need eye protection with a red light panel?+

No study settles this for panels. A 2026 case report described bilateral angle-closure glaucoma in a woman using a red light mask without eye protection, and its authors suggest eye protection should be considered. Shi and colleagues (2026) note visible red falls within the retinal-hazard region. We found no study of panel eyewear. Seek medical attention for eye pain or vision loss.

Is a longer or closer session better?+

Not necessarily. Huang and colleagues (2009) describe a biphasic dose response, where low levels of light can have a much better effect than higher levels. Harvard Health says scientists have not pinned down how long each session should last. We found no published session length for these panels, so follow the maker's directions.
