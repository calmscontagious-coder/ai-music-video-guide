What does the research say about training two days a week?

Does twice a week beat once a week?

The clearest answer comes from Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2016), who pooled ten studies on training frequency and muscle growth. Higher frequency produced a larger effect size than lower frequency, 0.49 ± 0.08 against 0.30 ± 0.07. Their conclusion: training twice a week promotes superior hypertrophic outcomes to once a week, so the major muscle groups should be trained at least twice a week. Whether three times beats twice, in their words, remains to be determined.

Does frequency still matter when the sets are equal?

Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019) then reviewed 25 studies and separated trials where weekly sets were matched between frequencies from those where they were not. On a volume-equated basis there was no significant difference between higher and lower frequency. In the non-equated studies higher frequency won, but the difference between 1 and 3+ days per week was modest. Their reading: frequency does not meaningfully change hypertrophy when volume is equated, so for a given weekly volume a person can pick a frequency on preference.

What do the public health guidelines ask for?

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, 2nd edition (2018) state that adults should do muscle-strengthening activities of moderate or greater intensity, involving all major muscle groups, on 2 or more days a week. The plain-language summary of the same guidelines puts it as muscle-strengthening activity at least 2 days each week. The World Health Organization 2020 guidelines (Bull et al., 2020) use the same wording, 2 or more days a week, and grade it a strong recommendation supported by moderate-certainty evidence; the WHO authors found no evidence of a dose-response association with higher volumes of muscle-strengthening activity.

Are dumbbells a compromise compared with machines?

No, as far as muscle size goes. Haugen et al. (2023) meta-analysed thirteen studies with a total sample of 1016 participants (789 men, 219 women). For hypertrophy the pooled difference between free weights and machines was an SMD of -0.055 (CI: -0.397, 0.287, p = 0.751), so no difference was detected; the interval is wide, and free weights in the included studies were not only dumbbells. Strength was modality-specific: free-weight tests favoured free-weight training (SMD: -0.210, CI: -0.391, -0.029, p = 0.023), so the authors put the choice down to preference and goals. One limit: only 5 of the thirteen studies measured hypertrophy, so the size comparison rests on a smaller pool.

A single trial shows the same picture in beginners. In Schwanbeck et al. (2020), Forty-six subjects (26 women; 22 ± 3 years) were randomised to free weights or machines for 8 weeks, each muscle group trained 2-3 times a week with 3-4 sets of 4-10 repetitions. Biceps and quadriceps muscle thickness increased with no difference between groups, and free-weight bench press and squat and Smith machine squat strength rose 11-19% in both groups; machine bench press strength rose more in the machine group (13.9 vs. 8.6%). The participants were young adults.

How many sets a week is the floor?

The time-efficient training review by Iversen et al. (2021) states that weekly training volume is more important than training frequency and recommends a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group, using a 6 to 15 RM loading range, with 15 to 40 repetitions acceptable if sets are taken to volitional failure. The same review recommends at least one leg pressing exercise, one upper-body pulling exercise and one upper-body pushing exercise, with machines or free weights by preference, and advises untrained people to rest 1 to 2 min between sets.

On sets per exercise, Krieger (2010) analysed 55 effect sizes from 19 groups across 8 studies. Multiple sets beat a single set (difference = 0.10 +/- 0.04; CI: 0.02, 0.19; p = 0.016), and the source describes a tendency for effect sizes to rise with set number: 0.24 for 1 set, 0.34 for 2-3 sets and 0.44 for 4-6 sets. The step from 2-3 sets to 4-6 sets was not significant (difference = 0.10 +/- 0.10; CI: -0.09, 0.30; p = 0.29). Overall, multiple sets were associated with 40% greater hypertrophy-related effect sizes than 1 set, in both trained and untrained subjects.

How do you reach the minimum weekly set count in two sessions?

Start from the floor of 4 weekly sets per muscle group. Across two sessions that is 2 sets per muscle group per session, so a short session covers it if every major muscle gets a compound exercise. We would set each exercise at 2-3 sets: in Krieger's meta-analysis 2-3 sets per exercise produced a larger effect size than 1 set (0.34 against 0.24), and the further step to 4-6 sets (0.44) was not statistically significant, though the estimates kept rising. Pick loads in the 6 to 15 RM range and stop sets with a repetition or two in reserve at first.

Cover the three essential patterns in both sessions: a leg press pattern (goblet squat or split squat), an upper-body pull (one-arm row) and an upper-body push (bench press or floor press), plus a hip hinge (Romanian deadlift) for the hamstrings and glutes. What does a full-body dumbbell session look like?

Pair opposing movements and alternate them: a push with a pull, and a quadriceps exercise with a hamstring exercise. This is antagonist pairing. Iversen and colleagues note that supersets roughly halve training time compared with traditional training while maintaining volume. The review advises against supersets for heavy squats and bench presses on safety grounds, so use pairing with moderate loads, keep the squat and press rests full if the weight is heavy, and read the time saving as a hypertrophy-oriented estimate based on a small number of studies. For beginners we keep a 1 to 2 min rest after each pair.

How do you progress over eight weeks?

Use double progression. Hold the dumbbell weight until you complete the top of your repetition target on every set of an exercise, then move up to the next dumbbell and drop back toward the bottom of the range. Within the 6 to 15 RM window that gives weeks of progress on one pair of adjustable dumbbells. Add load when you reach the top of the repetition range; if a session has time, a third set is a reasonable first addition.

Eight weeks is a realistic window: the Schwanbeck trial measured increases in biceps and quadriceps thickness after 8 weeks of training each muscle group 2-3 times a week in young adults, which is more volume than the template above. Log the load and repetitions for every set, and if an exercise stalls for two sessions, drop one set or one repetition target and build back.

The studies above recruited healthy adults; the Schwanbeck participants were 22 ± 3 years old. If you are older, pregnant, recovering from an injury, or have a heart, blood pressure or joint condition, get clearance from a qualified professional before loading a squat or a hinge, and ask for a technique check on both. A two-day plan is not the best fit for an experienced lifter chasing maximum growth. Iversen's review calls weekly volume more important than frequency, and two sessions mean each session must carry more sets, which lengthens it; trained people need rest intervals of at least 2 min by the Iversen recommendation. For that reader a 15-minute full-body plan run most days spreads the same sets more comfortably.

Who should start here: true beginners, people returning after a long break, and anyone whose week reliably has two training windows. Learn the four patterns with light dumbbells first. If leg size is the main goal, a beginner leg hypertrophy program is a better-targeted two-day option.

Frequently asked questions

Is a dumbbell only full body workout 2 days a week enough to build muscle?+

It can, if the sessions carry enough sets. A 2016 meta-analysis found training a muscle twice a week produced more growth than once, and a 2019 review found frequency stopped mattering once weekly sets were equal. Two full-body sessions that each carry 2-3 sets per compound exercise meet the minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group.

Do you lose anything by using dumbbells instead of machines?+

Not in muscle size. A 2023 meta-analysis of thirteen studies and 1016 participants found no difference in hypertrophy between free weights and machines. Strength gains were specific to the tool tested, so free-weight training improved free-weight lifts more. An 8-week trial in young adults found similar thickness gains with either, and strength gains specific to the test equipment.

How many sets should each session contain?+

Aim for 2-3 sets per exercise on four compound patterns: a squat, a hinge, a push and a pull. That gives every major muscle at least 2 sets per session and 4 or more a week, the minimum the time-efficient training review recommends. In the set-volume meta-analysis the step from 2-3 to 4-6 sets per exercise was not statistically significant, though the estimates kept rising.
