What does the research say about 15-minute training?

Can 15 minutes build muscle?

The most direct answer comes from a narrative review on time-efficient training. Iversen and colleagues (2021) write that "micro dosing", meaning "frequent training sessions of very short duration (e.g. 15 min), could be a viable alternative to traditional programs." They add: "There are few studies and results should be interpreted with caution." The review sets the floor a short session has to respect: "Weekly training volume is more important than training frequency", and it recommends a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group in a 6 to 15 RM loading range, or 15 to 40 repetitions if the set is taken to volitional failure.

The review also names the exercises to keep. Iversen et al. (2021) recommend "prioritizing bilateral, multi-joint exercises that include full dynamic movements" and, at minimum, "one leg pressing exercise (e.g. squats), one upper-body pulling exercise (e.g. pull-up) and one upper-body pushing exercise (e.g. bench press)." A narrative review is an expert synthesis, not a pooled analysis, so read these as reasoned recommendations, not effect sizes.

For strength, an overview of minimal-dose strategies helps. Nuzzo et al. (2024) report that programs of one set of coupled eccentric and concentric repetitions for four or more exercises per session, at ≥ 2 sessions per week, "improve muscle strength and other markers of physical fitness in both untrained and trained individuals". Strength gains in the exercise groups were "~ 20%", greater than typically observed in control groups; the authors note that "Few studies included control groups for comparison", so the comparison is indicative. That overview measured strength, not muscle size.

How do antagonist supersets fit enough work into 15 minutes?

A superset pairs two exercises back to back with little or no rest between them. A 2025 meta-analysis pooled 19 studies and 313 participants. Zhang et al. (2025) found supersets produced "a similar total number of repetitions [standardized mean differences (SMD) = - 0.03; p = 0.92] and volume load (SMD = 0.05; p = 0.86) with a shorter session duration and increased training efficiency (SMD = 1.74; p = 0.01)".

Over the long run the outcomes matched too. Zhang et al. (2025) report "similar chronic adaptations in maximal strength (SMD = 0.10; p = 0.36), strength endurance (SMD = 0.07; p = 0.81), and muscle hypertrophy (SMD = - 0.05; p = 0.87)". The 19 studies cover acute and chronic outcomes, the authors note "considerable variance in certain outcomes", and a non-significant difference is not proof the methods are identical. The costs are effort and recovery: supersets produced a "higher rating of perceived exertion (SMD = 0.77; p = 0.02)", and the authors conclude that "supersets generally induce higher internal loads, more severe muscle damage, and increased perceived exertion, potentially necessitating extended recovery times between sessions."

Pairing matters. The same review concludes that "agonist-antagonist supersets are more suitable for maintaining training volume, while similar biomechanical supersets concentrate stimulation on the same muscle group, compromising volume load." Iversen et al. (2021) make the same point about time: "supersets, drop sets and rest-pause training roughly halves training time compared to traditional training, while maintaining training volume." That is the mechanism that lets a 15 minute full body workout plan hold a useful number of sets.

Two limits from Iversen et al. (2021) belong here. They advise against drop sets, rest-pause and supersets on heavy compound free-weight lifts such as the squat and bench press, "Due to safety concerns", and describe these methods as primarily beneficial for hypertrophy rather than strength: supersets are "probably better at inducing hypertrophy than muscular strength, and more research is needed on longitudinal training effects."

How many sets a week do you still need?

Short sessions change how you reach the weekly total, not the total. The dose-response meta-analysis covered 34 treatment groups from 15 studies. Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017) found "Each additional set was associated with an increase in effect size (ES) of 0.023 corresponding to an increase in the percentage gain by 0.37%", and "the ES difference between higher and lower volumes was 0.241, which equated to a percentage gain difference of 3.9%." The split into under 5, 5 to 9 and 10 or more weekly sets per muscle "showed a trend for an effect of weekly sets (P = 0.074)", which is a trend, not a significant result.

Frequency, on its own, matters much less for muscle size. A second meta-analysis of 25 studies, Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019), found "no significant difference between higher and lower frequency on a volume-equated basis", with "strong evidence that resistance training frequency does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated." Where volume was not equated, higher frequency won, but the 1 versus 3+ days per week difference was modest. Strength and five-day schedules were not the question.

Put the papers together and the arithmetic of a 15 minute full body workout plan is simple. You need the 4 weekly sets per muscle group floor from Iversen et al. (2021), and more is better up the dose-response curve. The review says the floor can be met in one or several shorter sessions, so count sets across the week: "Training can be performed in one, or several shorter sessions - whatever suits the individual." In a do-it-yourself five-day version, one working set per muscle group in each session clears the floor across the week, and frequency made no meaningful difference to muscle size when volume was equated, per Schoenfeld et al. (2019).

For your own version, the minimum list from Iversen et al. (2021) is the template: one leg press pattern, one upper-body pull, one upper-body push, all bilateral and multi-joint, run as agonist-antagonist pairs, with the heavy squat and bench press kept out of the supersets. Let the week, not the session, hit the set floor.

How heavy and how many reps?

Use the loading range the review names: 6 to 15 RM, or 15 to 40 repetitions when the set is taken to volitional failure, per Iversen et al. (2021). Pick a load you can control on both phases, because the minimal-dose evidence in Nuzzo et al. (2024) is built on coupled eccentric and concentric repetitions, meaning each rep includes a controlled lowering as well as a lifting phase.

How long should you rest and warm up?

Iversen et al. (2021) advise untrained individuals to rest 1 to 2 min between sets and trained individuals ≥ 2 min. Our reading, not a statement in the review, is that in an antagonist superset the paired exercise doubles as rest for the first muscle, which is how the session fits. On warm-ups, the same authors "advise restricting the warm-up to exercise-specific warm-ups, and only prioritize stretching if the goal of training is to increase flexibility."

Who should not do this, and what should you check first?

Anyone new to lifting who has not been cleared to train: supersets raise perceived exertion and muscle damage, per Zhang et al. (2025), so sessions feel harder than their length suggests and may need longer recovery between them, which matters on a five-day schedule. Talk to a qualified professional first if you have a heart, blood pressure, joint or other medical condition, are pregnant, or are returning from injury. People chasing maximal strength: Iversen et al. (2021) give trained lifters rest intervals of "≥ 2 min", keep supersets off the heavy squat and bench press, and call these methods mainly a hypertrophy tool. Anyone who cannot train most days: in the do-it-yourself five-day version the weekly set count depends on showing up; miss two sessions and the volume floor is gone.

Check the evidence limits too. The superset meta-analysis is a 313-participant pool, read here from its abstract. The time-efficiency paper is a narrative review. The volume categories were a trend at P = 0.074, not a significant split. The minimal-dose overview reports strength, not muscle size. None of these studies tested this program.

If you can only lift twice a week, do not force five sessions; reach the weekly sets in fewer sessions. Our guide to a dumbbell-only full body workout two days a week covers that format and the program built for it.

Frequently asked questions

Can a 15-minute workout really build muscle?+

A review of time-efficient training says very short, frequent sessions could be a viable alternative to traditional programs, while noting there are few studies and results should be read with caution. It recommends a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group in a 6 to 15 RM range, and says the floor can be met in one or several shorter sessions.

Why use antagonist supersets instead of straight sets?+

A 2025 meta-analysis of 19 studies found supersets matched traditional training on repetitions, volume load, strength and hypertrophy while shortening the session. Pairing opposing muscles, such as a push with a pull, preserved volume best. The costs were higher perceived exertion, higher internal load and more muscle damage, which may call for longer recovery between sessions.

How many sets a week do I need for muscle growth?+

The dose-response meta-analysis found each added weekly set raised the effect size by 0.023, about 0.37% more gain, and higher-volume groups gained about 3.9 percentage points more muscle size than lower-volume groups (an effect size difference of 0.241). The time-efficient training review sets a floor of 4 weekly sets per muscle group, counted across the week.

Does training five days a week beat training twice a week?+

Not for muscle size by itself. A meta-analysis of 25 studies found no meaningful difference in muscle growth between higher and lower frequencies once weekly volume was equated; its non-equated comparison was 1 against 3 or more days per week, and strength was not the question. Five short sessions are one way to reach a weekly set target in 15-minute blocks.

How long should I rest between sets in a 15-minute session?+

The time-efficient training review advises 1 to 2 minute rest intervals for untrained lifters and at least 2 minutes for trained lifters. Our reading is that in an antagonist superset the paired exercise fills part of that rest, which is how the session stays short. Keep the warm-up to lighter sets of the exercise you are about to do.
