How do you set BFR cuff pressure?

Pressure is set relative to the person and the cuff, not copied from a chart. The 2019 methodology review in Frontiers in Physiology states: "It is therefore recommended to set pressure during BFR exercise based on measurement of AOP, with pressures ranging from 40 to 80% of AOP having evidence to support their efficacy" (Patterson et al. 2019). AOP is the arterial occlusion pressure: the cuff pressure at which blood stops flowing past the cuff, measured with a Doppler or a cuff system that reads it for you.

Cuff width changes that number. In 116 adults, "narrow: 235 (42) mmHg vs. wide: 144 (17) mmHg" was the occlusion pressure with a narrow versus a wide cuff (Loenneke et al. 2012). Material matters less once pressure is relative: nylon and elastic cuffs occluded at "139 (14) mmHg" and "246 (71) mmHg" at rest, yet "there were no differences in repetitions to failure, torque, muscle thickness, or muscle activation between the cuffs" when each was inflated to its own relative pressure (Buckner et al. 2017). That is why this calculator asks for your measured AOP with the cuff you will train with, and refuses to guess a mmHg figure from cuff width alone.

What does the evidence say low-load BFR does?

Across the meta-analyses in our notes, low-load lifting with cuffs grows muscle about as well as heavy lifting and builds strength somewhat less. The 2018 Sports Medicine meta-analysis found "similar effects between HL- and BFR-RT" for hypertrophy and "higher increases in muscle strength for HL- as compared with BFR-RT" (Lixandrão et al. 2018). In adults over 60, heavy training gave "a small increase in muscle strength (eight studies; SMD, −0.23 [−0.41; −0.05]) but not in muscle hypertrophy (six studies; (SMD, 0.08 [−0.22; 0.38])" compared with low-load BFR (Fabero-Garrido et al. 2022). For upper-body muscles above the cuff, the evidence is "of very low to low certainty" (Pavlou et al. 2023). Frequently asked questions

What is arterial occlusion pressure and how do I measure it?+

It is the cuff pressure at which arterial blood stops flowing past the cuff on that limb, on that day, with that cuff. It is measured by inflating the cuff slowly while a Doppler probe listens to the pulse below it, or by a cuff system with that function built in. Width, limb size and the cuff itself all change the number.

Can I just use a tight knee wrap and a perceived tightness of 7 out of 10?+

Wraps are a common practical method, but perceived tightness is not a measured pressure, and the research ranges are expressed as a share of measured occlusion pressure. If you use wraps, keep the load light, the reps in the 75-rep scheme, release between exercises, and stop on numbness. A measured cuff removes the guesswork.

How heavy should the weight be?+

Twenty to forty percent of your one-rep max for that exercise. The 2019 review suggests that range because it has consistently produced muscle adaptations with BFR. At the bottom of the range the review notes a higher pressure, around eighty percent of occlusion pressure, may be needed. Do not raise the load to make it feel like heavy training.

Is BFR safe?+

The review of the literature reports minimal adverse events related to clots and no clinically reported events, with risk concentrated in people who already carry clotting risk factors: prior clots, surgery, fractures, cancer, immobility, oral contraceptives, pregnancy, and inherited clotting conditions. Anyone in those groups needs medical clearance first. Numbness means the pressure is wrong.

How often can I train with cuffs?+

The published guideline is two to three sessions a week for programs longer than three weeks, or one to two sessions a day for short blocks of one to three weeks. Keep each exercise under five to ten minutes of restriction and let blood flow return between exercises. Treat it as a tool alongside normal training, not a replacement for every session.
