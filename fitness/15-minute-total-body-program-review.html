Does the method hold up?

The 15-Minute Series, Total Body Program (Antagonist Splits) is marketed on two ideas: antagonist supersets save time without costing muscle, and 15-minute sessions are enough. The page's title says "Antagonist Splits" and its lessons describe circuits of six lettered exercises; it does not say how the exercises are paired or sequenced. If they are run as agonist-antagonist supersets, the Zhang et al. evidence below applies; if they are run as a six-exercise circuit, it applies only loosely. The research gives numbers on the first idea and a caveat on the second.

Do supersets match straight sets?

The Zhang et al. 2025 meta-analysis pooled "Nineteen studies involving 313 participants" comparing superset and traditional prescriptions. Supersets produced "a similar total number of repetitions [standardized mean differences (SMD) = - 0.03; p = 0.92] and volume load (SMD = 0.05; p = 0.86) with a shorter session duration and increased training efficiency (SMD = 1.74; p = 0.01)". Chronic results were also level: "similar chronic adaptations in maximal strength (SMD = 0.10; p = 0.36), strength endurance (SMD = 0.07; p = 0.81), and muscle hypertrophy (SMD = - 0.05; p = 0.87)". The cost was a "higher rating of perceived exertion (SMD = 0.77; p = 0.02)". The abstract's results show similar creatine kinase after sessions (SMD = 0.22; p = 0.36), while its conclusions say supersets generally induce "more severe muscle damage" and may need "extended recovery times between sessions". The authors add that "agonist-antagonist supersets are more suitable for maintaining training volume, while similar biomechanical supersets concentrate stimulation on the same muscle group, compromising volume load". Agonist-antagonist pairing is the arrangement the evidence favours.

Is 15 minutes enough?

The Iversen et al. 2021 narrative review on time-efficient training is the closest match to this product. It states that "Weekly training volume is more important than training frequency and we recommend performing a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group using a 6 to 15 RM loading range". It states that supersets and similar techniques "roughly halves training time compared to traditional training, while maintaining training volume". On the session length itself the wording is cautious: "So-called micro dosing, i.e. frequent training sessions of very short duration (e.g. 15 min), could be a viable alternative to traditional programs". "Could be" is the authors' phrase; the review is narrative, not a meta-analysis, and the direct evidence on 15-minute sessions is thin. The same review advises against supersetting heavy squat and bench work, and it advises untrained individuals to schedule rest intervals of 1 to 2 min and trained individuals 2 min or more. The Week 1 - Thursday circuit lists a hex-bar deadlift and barbell back squats; the page does not say how heavy they are or whether they are paired, so use loads you can control without a spotter.

Does a small dose still move strength?

The Nuzzo et al. 2024 overview of minimal-dose resistance exercise found that one set of four or more exercises, done at least twice a week, improved strength compared with no exercise, though few studies included control groups. The two-day plan's set counts are not printed on its page, so we cannot say it matches that protocol. In its words, programs "consisting of one set of coupled eccentric-concentric repetitions for four or more exercises per session (≥ 2 sessions per week) improve muscle strength and other markers of physical fitness in both untrained and trained individuals", with "strength gains observed in the resistance exercise groups (~ 20%)" above control groups. Five short sessions a week sit well above a minimal dose; the question is whether the program clears the volume floor.

Does volume still matter in a short session?

Yes. The Schoenfeld et al. 2017 dose-response meta-analysis, covering "34 treatment groups from 15 studies", found that "Each additional set was associated with an increase in effect size (ES) of 0.023 corresponding to an increase in the percentage gain by 0.37%". The three-level split (<5, 5-9 and 10+ weekly sets per muscle) "showed a trend for an effect of weekly sets (P = 0.074)", which did not reach significance. For this product, the lettered exercises and finishers on each day are the whole dose; whether they add up to the minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group depends on set counts inside the lessons, which the page does not print.
