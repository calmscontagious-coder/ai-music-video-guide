Does the method hold up?

Is twice a week enough?

Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2016) pooled ten studies and found higher training frequency associated with a greater effect size than lower frequency, 0.49 ± 0.08 against 0.30 ± 0.07. On a volume-equated basis, they concluded, training twice a week promotes superior hypertrophic outcomes to once a week. The follow-up, Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019), reviewed 25 studies and found no significant difference between higher and lower frequency on a volume-equated basis. Both reviews count how often each muscle group is trained per week, not how many sessions you attend. The 2-day program trains every region twice a week; the 5-day body-part rotation often trains a region once in a given week, so session count itself tells you little. Weekly volume matters more than frequency in these reviews, and neither page states set counts, so you cannot check it before buying.

How many sets does a beginner need?

Krieger (2010) pooled 55 effect sizes from 8 studies and found multiple sets per exercise associated with a larger effect size than a single set per exercise (difference = 0.10 +/- 0.04; p = 0.016). Effect sizes rose from 0.24 for 1 set per exercise to 0.34 for 2-3 sets and 0.44 for 4-6 sets. The two steps are about equal, +0.10 each. The first (1 versus 2-3 sets per exercise) was reported as a trend (p = 0.09), significant only by a permutation test; the second (2-3 versus 4-6 sets) was not significant (p = 0.29, CI: -0.09, 0.30). That is a tendency, not proof that 2-3 sets are enough or that more is no better.

Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld and Fimland (2021), a narrative review on time-efficient training, recommend a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group (a weekly total per muscle, a different unit from Krieger's sets per exercise) in a 6 to 15 RM loading range, and state that weekly volume is more important than frequency. They add that supersets, drop sets and rest-pause training roughly halve training time while maintaining volume, but advise against these methods for heavy compound free-weight exercises such as the squat and bench press. The 5-day page labels its sessions "Regional-Match Supersets" and "Double Dropsets", so that caution is worth knowing before you load a heavy press. For untrained people they advise 1 to 2 min rest intervals.

Do free weights work as well as machines?

The 2-day program uses dumbbells, a bench and bodyweight; the 5-day program is mostly dumbbells but names a few cable lessons. Haugen and colleagues (2023) pooled thirteen studies with 1016 participants aged 18 to 60, comparing free weights with machines. Muscle hypertrophy was measured in only 5 of the 13 studies, and no difference was detected between free weights and machines (hypertrophy SMD: -0.055, CI: -0.397, 0.287, p = 0.751), with a wide interval. Strength in free-weight tests increased more with free-weight training (SMD: -0.210, p = 0.023). Treat that as no difference detected, not proof of equality.

One caution on all of this: these are reviews of mixed populations, not trials of either product. They inform the design choices; they do not measure what either course delivers.

Two full-body sessions work when each one carries enough sets. Attend both days and work every circuit exercise.
