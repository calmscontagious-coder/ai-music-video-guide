What does the research say?

What a cuff has to do

Loenneke et al. (2013) put the cuff's job in one sentence: "The pressure applied should be high enough to occlude venous return from the muscle but low enough to maintain arterial inflow into the muscle."

So a cuff has three jobs: fit the limb, hold a pressure between venous and arterial occlusion for the whole set, and tell you what that pressure is as a fraction of your own arterial occlusion pressure (AOP). Patterson et al. (2019) recommend setting pressure "based on measurement of AOP, with pressures ranging from 40 to 80% of AOP having evidence to support their efficacy". A band that cannot report a pressure cannot meet that recommendation.

Why cuff width is the first spec to check

Loenneke et al. (2012) tested one hundred and sixteen subjects (53 males, 63 females) in a single laboratory session, on the thigh in healthy adults averaging 22 years, with 5 cm and 13.5 cm cuffs, and found "Significant differences were observed between cuff type and arterial occlusion (narrow: 235 (42) mmHg vs. wide: 144 (17) mmHg; p = 0.001, Cohen's D = 2.52)".

Patterson et al. (2019) add a warning for shoppers: "Although lower pressures can be used with a wider cuff, this does not necessarily equate to a safer stimulus". What matters is where the number sits relative to your AOP with that cuff.

The widths in the Patterson et al. (2019) guideline table are "5 (small), 10 or 12 (medium), 17 or 18 cm (large)". A product with another width, or none stated, cannot be mapped onto the evidence until the seller confirms the width; ask before buying.

Does the material matter?

Buckner et al. (2017) compared inflatable nylon (5 cm) and elastic (3 cm) cuffs on the upper arm and found "Resting arterial occlusion pressure was different between the nylon [139 (14) mmHg] and elastic [246 (71) mmHg, p < 0.001] cuffs". Yet when each was inflated relative to its own occlusion pressure, "there were no differences in repetitions to failure, torque, muscle thickness, or muscle activation between the cuffs".

Patterson et al. (2019) reach the same reading of the literature: "the material of the cuff does not appear to impact the outcomes of BFR-RE." Buy for fit, width and pressure control, not for a material claim.

Why an mmHg setting from one cuff means nothing on another

The figures above show the same dial number can be a different fraction of AOP on different cuffs. Loenneke et al. (2013) showed what that means for a fixed number: "In our narrow (5 cm) cuff dataset of 83 participants, 19 of them would be at or above their respective arterial occlusion pressure at an arbitrary BFR pressure of 200 mmHg." That was measured at rest, supine, on the leg. Their recommendation: "Pressures should be relative to the individual just as the loads lifted in those studies were relative to the individual." Both Loenneke et al. (2012) and Loenneke et al. (2013) suggest basing lower-body pressure on thigh circumference, and Loenneke et al. (2013) add that "These recommendations are only applicable to the lower body and may not necessarily reflect what occurs in the upper body."

Patterson et al. (2019) report the cost of ignoring this: "a wide range of pressures applied by practitioners that resulted in unintended consequences such as a large incidence of numbness following BFR."

Setting the pressure once you own a gauge

Two steps. First, find your AOP for that cuff on that limb. AOP is measured with Doppler ultrasound in the studies; ask a trained clinician or a practitioner who uses a Doppler to measure it and tell you the pressure for your cuff. Second, train at a fraction of it. Patterson et al. (2019) support "40 to 80% of AOP", and note that "When loads used are at the bottom end of this recommendation (e.g., ∼20% of 1-RM), a higher pressure (∼80% AOP) may be required necessary to elicit muscle growth". Patterson et al. (2019) add that further study is warranted to confirm this, so use the clinician-set pressure. Re-measure when you change cuff or limb.

The set scheme the cuff has to survive

Buy for the session you will run. The Patterson et al. (2019) guideline table gives a load of 20 to 40% 1RM, 2 to 4 sets, 75 repetitions as 30, 15, 15, 15 or sets to failure, pressure at 40 to 80% AOP, rest of 30 to 60 s between sets, restriction time of 5 to 10 min per exercise with reperfusion between exercises, and a frequency of 2 to 3 times a week for more than 3 weeks, or 1 to 2 times per day for 1 to 3 weeks. A cuff must hold its pressure for the full 5 to 10 min of restriction and deflate fast between exercises.

Where the cuff goes

Pavlou et al. (2023) summarise placement as: "Usually, the BFR cuff is applied at the deltoid tuberosity for an upper limb application and at the gluteal fold for a lower limb application". An arm set is sized for the first position only.

One caution on the upper body. Pavlou et al. (2023) searched 346 articles, included 9 studies and judged the evidence for muscles proximal to an upper-arm cuff (chest and shoulder) to be of very low to low certainty; it does not rate training of the arm muscles under the cuff.

Who should not do this, and what should you check first?

Patterson et al. (2019) report that "the totality of the literature reveals minimal adverse events pertaining to VTE and clinically reported events have not been reported." The same paper lists the risk factors; anyone with one of them should get clearance from a qualified clinician before buying a cuff:

  • Medical conditions: "major orthopedic surgery, major general surgery, lower extremity paralysis due to spinal cord injury, pelvic, hip or long bone fractures, poly-trauma and cancer".
  • Additional factors: "a history of prior VTE, obesity, immobility, oral contraceptives, family history of VTE, physical inactivity and genetic conditions that affect blood clotting".
  • "Pregnancy carries an elevated risk both in the perinatal and post-natal periods".

Patterson et al. (2019) note isolated case reports of rhabdomyolysis with BFR resistance exercise, with an incidence in published literature of 0.07 to 0.2%; cases in general are typically associated with loads that greatly exceed the person's fitness. Patterson et al. (2019) list numbness as an unintended consequence seen when practitioners applied a wide range of pressures; stop and ask a clinician if it happens.

The pre-purchase checklist

  • Width stated in centimetres, and inside the 5, 10 or 12, 17 or 18 cm bands from the Patterson et al. (2019) table; if the listing does not state one, ask the seller.
  • A gauge, or a readable pressure display, so you can find AOP and set a fraction of it.
  • A bladder that holds through a 5 to 10 min restriction window.
  • Sizing for your limb at the deltoid tuberosity or gluteal fold, the placements in Pavlou et al. (2023).
  • A plan for getting AOP measured by a trained person, not just a suggested mmHg number; a fixed number is the mistake Loenneke et al. (2013) documented.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a pneumatic cuff, or will elastic bands work for BFR?+

Elastic bands give no pressure reading, so you cannot set a fraction of your arterial occlusion pressure or repeat it next session. The published guidance sets pressure at 40 to 80% of AOP, which needs a gauge and a measured AOP, and the trials cited in this guide used inflatable cuffs only. A pneumatic cuff is the type that lets you follow that guidance.

What cuff width should I buy for BFR training?+

Pick a width that appears in the research: 5 cm (small), 10 or 12 cm (medium), or 17 or 18 cm (large). Wider cuffs occlude at lower pressure, narrower cuffs need more, so width changes every number you will use. A product with no stated width cannot be mapped to the published pressure guidance, so ask the seller for the width before buying.

Can I use the mmHg setting from a study or a friend's cuff?+

No. Occlusion pressure depends on the cuff width, material and your limb. In one dataset, measured at rest, supine, on the leg, a fixed 200 mmHg on a narrow cuff put 19 of 83 people at or above full arterial occlusion. Have a trained person measure your AOP with your own cuff on the limb you are training, then set 40 to 80% of that reading.

Is nylon worse than elastic for a BFR cuff?+

Not in the acute measures that have been tested. A study comparing nylon and elastic cuffs of similar width found different resting occlusion pressures but no difference in repetitions to failure, torque, muscle thickness or activation once each cuff was set relative to its own occlusion pressure. Choose on fit, width and whether you can read the pressure.

Who should get medical clearance before buying BFR bands?+

Anyone with a clotting risk factor: major orthopedic or general surgery, fractures of the pelvis, hip or long bones, lower-limb paralysis from spinal cord injury, cancer, a personal or family history of VTE, obesity, immobility, oral contraceptive use, physical inactivity, or a genetic clotting condition. Pregnancy also carries elevated risk. Talk to a qualified clinician before the first session, not after.
