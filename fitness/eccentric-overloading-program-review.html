Does the method hold up?

The library sells techniques for making the lowering phase heavier than the lifting phase, or for lowering only. The research splits into two questions: does eccentric work beat concentric work, and does adding eccentric load to ordinary sets beat the same sets at a constant load. The answers differ.

Eccentric versus concentric training

Roig and colleagues (2009) pooled twenty randomised controlled trials in healthy adults and found that "when eccentric exercise was performed at higher intensities compared with concentric training, total strength and eccentric strength increased more significantly". High-intensity eccentric training was also "more effective in promoting increases in muscle mass measured as muscle girth", with a trend toward larger cross-sectional area on MRI or CT. The authors tie the advantage to "the higher loads developed during eccentric contractions", so the win belongs to heavier eccentric loading, not to eccentric movement at a matched load.

Douglas and colleagues (2017) reviewed 40 studies of eccentric training lasting at least 4 weeks in healthy adults aged 17-35 years. Eccentric training "elicits greater improvements in muscle strength, although in a largely mode-specific manner", is "at least as effective as other modalities in increasing muscle cross-sectional area", and "eccentric loads not constrained by concentric strength" appear superior for strength, power and speed. The population limit is the one to notice: the evidence is from young, healthy adults, and the review gives no pooled effect size.

Eccentric overload on a flywheel

Maroto-Izquierdo and colleagues (2017) meta-analysed flywheel eccentric-overload training against conventional resistance training: 9 studies, 276 subjects and 92 effect sizes, with an overall pooled estimate of 0.63 (95% CI 0.49-0.76) favouring the flywheel in strength, power, hypertrophy, jump height and running speed. The cautions: the 92 effect sizes come from only 9 studies, so they may not be independent, and the device was a flywheel. Nothing on the library page uses one, so this result supports the general idea rather than these exact techniques.

Accentuated eccentric loading versus constant load

This is the comparison that matches most of the library, and it is the least flattering. Zhang and colleagues (2026) pooled forty-nine studies with 773 participants comparing accentuated eccentric loading with constant-load resistance training. Chronic outcomes were similar: maximal concentric strength SMD = 0.12 (p = 0.41), maximal eccentric strength SMD = 0.19 (p = 0.58), maximal isometric strength SMD = 0.03 (p = 0.93) and muscle cross-sectional area SMD = -0.06 (p = 0.84). Muscle fascicle length came out at SMD = 0.90 (p = 0.17), a large point estimate that did not reach significance.

What accentuated loading did change was the acute response. The same review found higher blood lactate immediately post-intervention (SMD = 0.44; p = 0.03), higher growth hormone (SMD = 0.50; p = 0.01), more eccentric-phase muscle electrical activity (SMD = 0.37; p = 0.01) and a much higher rating of perceived exertion (SMD = 1.72; p = 0.01). The authors conclude that the method "increases metabolic stress and perceived effort, implying a need for longer, more frequent inter-set rests and longer between-session recovery", and that "given the lack of evidence for superior chronic benefits in strength or muscle architecture over constant-load RT, practitioners should consider these factors carefully".

Our reading, applied to the product: the Eccentric-Only lessons (Nordic curls, Copenhagen adductions, jumping pull-up negatives) sit closest to the eccentric-versus-concentric evidence, where heavier eccentric loading showed an advantage. The Self-Assist, Partner-Assist and Concentric-Cheating lessons are accentuated eccentric loading, where the newest meta-analysis (49 studies, 773 participants) found no evidence of a chronic advantage and a harder session. Both readings come from abstracts of reviews; none of the studies tested the lessons in this library. Buy it for variety and skill, not for a growth edge the evidence does not show. Who is it for, and who should skip it?

It fits a lifter past the first year who already follows a program, has stalled on a lift or a muscle, and wants a demonstrated technique to overload the lowering phase there. It fits a gym member, since cables, a preacher bar, a leg press and seated machines appear throughout, and a training partner pair best of all, because the Partner-Assist lessons (curls with a broom, overhead extensions with rags, push-ups with a plate) need a second person.

Skip it if any of these apply:

  • You are in your first year of lifting. The 2017 Douglas review covered healthy adults aged 17-35 years, none of the cited reviews reports results for beginners, and accentuated eccentric loading raised perceived exertion (SMD = 1.72) and recovery needs.
  • You need a plan. The page sets no weeks, sessions, sets or reps. A buyer who wants to be told what to do on Monday should pick a program from our fitness program comparison, not this library.
  • You train alone with dumbbells only. DB lessons exist (curls, presses, lateral raises), but a large share of the list needs a partner, a cable stack or a machine.
  • You have a tendon, joint or back complaint. Heavier eccentric loads are the mechanism here, and the review authors call for longer rests and recovery; get clearance from a qualified professional first.
  • You dislike paying for unfinished content. The Overview lists "A Work In Progress!" and the page does not say what is still to come or when.
