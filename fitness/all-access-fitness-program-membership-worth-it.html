What does the research say?

The Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans (2018) say adults should do muscle-strengthening activity involving all major muscle groups on 2 or more days a week. The World Health Organization guideline (Bull 2020) says the same, as a strong recommendation on moderate-certainty evidence. Neither has an end date, and an 8-week program covers a fraction of a year.

The second point is progression. Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger (2017) pooled 34 groups from 15 studies and found a graded dose-response between weekly set volume and muscle growth: each additional set was associated with an effect size increase of 0.023, or a 0.37% larger gain, and the gap between higher and lower volumes was an effect size of 0.241, a 3.9% difference. The split into fewer than 5, 5-9 and 10+ weekly sets per muscle was only a trend (P = 0.074), so the thresholds are soft. The practical reading: a lifter who keeps growing needs more work over time than their first program, which is where a library of programs at different volumes earns its place.

How you spread that volume across the week matters less. Schoenfeld, Grgic and Krieger (2019) reviewed 25 studies and found no significant difference between higher and lower frequency when weekly volume was equated; unequated, higher frequency won, but the gap between 1 and 3+ days per week was modest. The WHO authors found no dose-response between higher volumes of muscle-strengthening activity and health benefits, so the volume argument is about muscle size, not health.

On what a program must contain, Iversen, Norum, Schoenfeld and Fimland (2021) recommend bilateral, multi-joint exercises with full dynamic movements, at minimum one leg pressing, one upper-body pulling and one upper-body pushing exercise, a minimum of 4 weekly sets per muscle group in a 6 to 15 RM loading range, and rest intervals of 1 to 2 min for untrained lifters and 2 min or more for trained lifters. Check any program you pay for against that list; this is a narrative review, expert synthesis rather than a pooled estimate.

How do you run it?

A membership beats single programs only if you use it as a sequence. This is how we would run the All-Access Premium Membership.

  1. Start at your level. A beginner starts with the Total Leg (Beginner) program or the 2X / Week dumbbell program, both 2 sessions a week over 8 weeks, which meets the 2 or more days a week floor in the federal guideline (2018). See our beginner leg hypertrophy program guide.
  2. Check each program against the minimum list. A leg press pattern, an upper-body pull, an upper-body push, and the 4 weekly sets per muscle that Iversen and colleagues (2021) set as the floor.
  3. Step up volume between programs. The 2017 dose-response review is the reason to move from a 2-session program to a 5 strength + 1 cardio program later: more weekly sets, not more days for their own sake; the 2019 frequency review leaves the day count to preference.
  4. Use the short formats when time is the constraint. The 15-Minute Series is structured as circuits, per its curriculum. Iversen and colleagues (2021) say supersets, drop sets and rest-pause training roughly halve training time at equal volume; they do not review circuit programs, so the match is loose. They also say very short sessions (for example 15 min) could be a viable alternative, based on few studies, to be interpreted with caution. See our 15-minute full body workout plan.
  5. Use the technique libraries as reference, not programming. The Eccentric-Overloading library has 42 lessons across 12 modules by muscle; the Regional Hypertrophy library has 272 lessons across 47 muscle-region modules. Neither is a schedule.

Who should not buy a membership, and what should you check first?

Skip the membership if:

  • You have never finished a structured program. Buy one at $149.00 or $199.00 and complete it first.
  • One program covers your goal, such as the 8-week Total Core program at $99.00, as shown on its product page on November 3, 2026.
  • You cannot hold 2 or more days a week for the year. Below that floor, no library of programs helps.

Before paying, check three things on the product page. First, which billing option you are selecting: the premium page lists two yearly prices, $660.00 and $564.00, two 3-month prices and several monthly variants without a plan name or any note on which is current, and the basic page shows $71.88 / year beside $83.95 / year. We cannot say which applies at checkout; the product page is the only price that counts. Second, whether the lists still hold the 30 items and 13 items we counted on November 3, 2026. Third, the refund terms: the membership pages we read stated none.

The premium bundle includes blood flow restriction programs, which partially restrict arterial inflow and fully restrict venous outflow in the working muscle. Patterson and colleagues (2019) cite these general risk factors for venous thromboembolism: major orthopedic surgery, major general surgery, lower extremity paralysis due to spinal cord injury, pelvic, hip or long bone fractures, poly-trauma and cancer, plus a history of prior venous thromboembolism, obesity, immobility, oral contraceptives, family history of venous thromboembolism, physical inactivity and genetic conditions that affect blood clotting. Pregnancy carries an elevated risk in the perinatal and post-natal periods. Because BFR restricts venous outflow, ask a doctor whether any apply to you before starting a BFR program in the bundle.
