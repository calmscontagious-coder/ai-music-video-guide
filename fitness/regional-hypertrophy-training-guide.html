What does regional hypertrophy training mean?

A muscle is not one uniform block. The biceps, triceps and quadriceps each have proximal, middle and distal regions, and some have separate heads with different attachments. When researchers measure cross-sectional area at several points along a muscle after a training block, growth is often larger at one site than another. That is regional, or non-uniform, hypertrophy.

The training idea built on that observation is that exercise choice, range of motion, or the muscle length you load at can bias where growth happens. Two mechanisms get proposed: activation, where some exercises recruit one region harder than another, and muscle length, where loading a stretched muscle may produce more growth, possibly at particular sites. The question for your programming is how big the effect is.

What does the research say?

Muscles do grow non-uniformly, and activation predicts where

The clearest demonstration is Wakahara and colleagues (2013), who trained twelve young men on a multijoint elbow-extension exercise for 12 weeks, three days per week and measured both activation in the first session and cross-sectional change afterwards. The percent activated area of the triceps brachii in the first session was significantly higher in the middle regions than in the most proximal region, and the relative change in cross-sectional area after the intervention was also significantly greater in the middle regions than the most proximal region. The authors conclude that non-uniform hypertrophy is due to region-specific activation during the session.

Two limits apply. It is a 12-man training study of one exercise in one muscle, with no comparison group reported in the abstract, so it shows the pattern exists, not that you can move it. And it compares regions within one condition, not two exercises aimed at different regions.

Longer muscle length: a signal in the reviews, trivial in the meta-analysis

The direct test of "train long to grow a region" is the Bayesian meta-analysis by Varovic and colleagues (2025 meta-analysis). Twelve studies in young adults were pooled, and the regional effect of training at longer versus shorter mean muscle length was estimated at three sites. Proximal (25% muscle length) gave an SMD of 0.05 [95% QI: -0.07, 0.16], mid-belly (50% muscle length) 0.07 [95% QI: -0.02, 0.15], and distal (75% muscle length) 0.09 [95% QI: -0.01, 0.19]. Every interval crosses zero; the authors conclude that training at longer and shorter mean muscle lengths produces similar hypertrophic effects.

They also flag a limit: the "shorter" and "longer" conditions in the included studies differed by an average of only 21.8% mean muscle length. A larger contrast might show a larger effect; the data cannot say.

A separate systematic review, Wolf and colleagues (2025), asked whether longer-muscle-length training causes more longitudinal growth, meaning more sarcomeres in series. Eight studies with a combined sample of 120 met inclusion. The authors suggest muscle size and fascicle length increases may be greater after longer-length training, but call the evidence largely mixed: no study estimated serial sarcomere number directly, and all but one used linear extrapolation to estimate fascicle length, which has questionable validity. There is no pooled effect size, so this is weaker evidence than the meta-analysis above.

A direct regional trial in trained lifters

A direct regional trial in trained lifters is Varovic and colleagues (2025 trial), which compared long-muscle-length isometric training against full range of motion isotonic training. Twenty-three healthy, resistance-trained men and women completed a 6-week, twice-per-week intervention, and the two conditions produced similar quadriceps hypertrophy overall. Long-length isometrics may have produced a superior effect in the proximal anterior thigh, but the authors say uncertainty in the estimates precludes a definitive conclusion. A hint in one region, in one small trial.

Range of motion: full versus partial

Range of motion has the most data. The meta-analysis by Wolf and colleagues (2023) found a trivial SMD of 0.12 (95% CI: -0.02, 0.26) in favour of full range of motion over partial range of motion. A sub-group analysis pointed the other way for partials done at long muscle lengths, with an estimate of -0.28 (95% CI: -0.81, 0.16) favouring lengthened partials over full range, again with an interval that crosses zero. No clear difference appeared between upper-body and lower-body adaptations, and the authors summarise the differences as trivial to small.

The systematic review by Kassiano and colleagues (2023) covered eleven studies region by region. Full range and partials in the initial, stretched part of the movement produced greater hypertrophy of the rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, biceps brachii, and brachialis distal sites than partials in the final, shortened part, with between-group effect sizes of 0.20-0.90. One exception: partials in the middle part of the movement produced greater triceps brachii hypertrophy than full range, between-group ES 1.21. Their conclusion is that training at a longer muscle length, by either route, tends to give better growth in the quadriceps, biceps and triceps. These are individual-study effect sizes, not a pooled estimate.

The direct test in trained people is the trial by Wolf and colleagues (2025). Thirty healthy, resistance-trained participants had one arm assigned to lengthened partials and the other to full range of motion, two sessions per week, four exercises per session, four sets per exercise. Muscle thickness and unilateral lat pulldown 10-repetition-maximum improvements were similar between conditions. The authors advise emphasising the stretched position, by full range or lengthened partials, in upper-body training.

In sum: non-uniform growth is real, training at long muscle lengths looks at least as good as shorter lengths for most muscles studied (with a triceps exception in one review, where mid-range partials gave a between-group ES of 1.21), and steering growth to a region through muscle length is, on the pooled numbers, trivial.

How do you run regional hypertrophy training in practice?

The practical version is not a separate method. It is a set of exercise-selection rules layered on a normal program.

Keep the volume, frequency and progression you already use

The lengthened-partials trial matched total sets and frequency across conditions (two sessions per week, four exercises, four sets per exercise), so regional choices there sat inside an unchanged structure. Keep yours the same way. If you need a baseline, our fitness program comparison lists programs by sessions per week and length.

Load the stretched position for each target muscle

This is the one rule supported by Kassiano (2023) and the Wolf (2025) trial; Wolf (2023) is broadly consistent. In practice that means choosing the version of an exercise that loads the muscle while it is stretched, for example the bottom of a leg extension or curl; the exact joint angles are exercise-specific and the cited studies do not prescribe them. Full range of motion covers this by default; lengthened partials are the other route.

Rotate exercises per region, but expect the regional effect to be small

Because Wakahara (2013) found that the regions activated most in a session were the regions that grew most in one exercise and one muscle, a second exercise per muscle may help keep a region from being consistently under-recruited. That is a hedge against a lagging region, not a way to sculpt one; the Varovic (2025) meta-analysis is the reason to keep expectations modest.

Who should not do this, and what should you check first?

This is ordinary resistance training with attention to range of motion, so it carries the same screening needs as any lifting program, plus one.

  • Loading a muscle at long length puts the joint at end range under load. Anyone with a current joint injury, recent surgery, or a diagnosed tendinopathy at that joint should get clearance from a qualified professional first, and start lighter than their mid-range working weight.
  • The meta-analysis pooled young adults and the trials used trained men and women; the sources do not address older adults, adolescents or people with chronic conditions, so do not assume the findings transfer.
  • Beginners gain from any sensible program and do not need regional targeting; a structured plan matters more at that stage.
  • If pain, numbness, or joint swelling appears at end range, stop that exercise and get it assessed. Wolf (2023) notes partial range of motion may be an alternative where injury prevents full range; ask a qualified professional which range suits your injury.

Frequently asked questions

Can you grow one part of a muscle more than another?+

Yes, muscles grow unevenly along their length, and one study found the middle regions of the triceps were both activated more in a session and grew more than the most proximal region. What is not well supported is steering that growth on purpose by muscle length: the pooled effect across twelve studies was trivial, with every interval crossing zero.

Are lengthened partials better than full range of motion?+

In a trial of thirty trained lifters, lengthened partials and full range produced similar gains in muscle thickness and lat pulldown 10-rep-max (strength-endurance) over two sessions a week. A meta-analysis found a trivial edge for full range overall and a small, uncertain edge for lengthened partials in one sub-group. Either works; the stretched position is the common factor.

Does training at long muscle lengths make a muscle longer?+

A systematic review of eight studies with a combined sample of 120 suggested fascicle length may increase more after longer-length training, but the authors called the evidence mixed. No study measured sarcomere number directly, and most estimated fascicle length by extrapolation, which the review says has questionable validity.

Is a library organized by muscle region a complete program?+

No. The library reviewed here holds 47 muscle-region modules and 272 lessons but no training days, no week count and no sessions-per-week figure, and the page lists 0 h of video. Use it to choose exercises inside a program you already run. It does not set your volume, frequency or progression.

Who benefits most from regional exercise selection?+

Intermediate and advanced lifters who already train each muscle with adequate volume and have a region that lags. This is a judgment, not a finding of the studies. The trials were run in young and resistance-trained adults, so there is no direct evidence for older adults or people with chronic conditions.

What should I check before adding deep-stretch loading?+

Loading a muscle at long length puts the joint at end range under weight. Anyone with a current joint injury, recent surgery or a diagnosed tendinopathy at that joint should get clearance from a qualified professional first, start lighter than their mid-range working weight, and stop any exercise that causes pain, numbness or swelling.
