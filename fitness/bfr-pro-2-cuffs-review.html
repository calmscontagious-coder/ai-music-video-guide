Does the method hold up?

Why a readable pressure is the whole point

Patterson et al. (2019) define the method as one "partially restricting arterial inflow and fully restricting venous outflow in working musculature during exercise". Hitting that window needs a known pressure. The same paper states: "It is therefore recommended to set pressure during BFR exercise based on measurement of AOP, with pressures ranging from 40 to 80% of AOP having evidence to support their efficacy." AOP is the arterial occlusion pressure, measured per person, per cuff, per limb. A cuff that cannot report a pressure cannot be set to a fraction of AOP. An inflatable cuff with a gauge can, which is the category the BFR Pro 2.0 cuffs are sold in; the Amazon listing confirms a pump with a built-in sphygmomanometer.

On fixed numbers, Loenneke et al. (2013) looked at a fixed setting in their own data and reported, "In our narrow (5 cm) cuff dataset of 83 participants, 19 of them would be at or above their respective arterial occlusion pressure at an arbitrary BFR pressure of 200 mmHg." Their conclusion: "Pressures should be relative to the individual just as the loads lifted in those studies were relative to the individual." Their scope note: "These recommendations are only applicable to the lower body and may not necessarily reflect what occurs in the upper body." Patterson et al. (2019) cite their earlier practitioner survey on the practical cost of guessing: "there was a wide range of pressures applied by practitioners that resulted in unintended consequences such as a large incidence of numbness following BFR."

Why width is the first thing to check before buying

Loenneke et al. (2012) measured arterial occlusion in one hundred and sixteen people (53 males, 63 females) and found "Significant differences were observed between cuff type and arterial occlusion (narrow: 235 (42) mmHg vs. wide: 144 (17) mmHg; p = 0.001, Cohen's D = 2.52)." Their reading: "Wide BFR cuffs restrict arterial blood flow at a lower pressure than narrow BFR cuffs". The 2013 paper describes this dataset as 5 and 13.5 cm cuffs.

Patterson et al. (2019) sort cuffs into width classes in their guideline table: "5 (small), 10 or 12 (medium), 17 or 18 cm (large)", and add a caution for anyone reading "wide" as "gentle": "Although lower pressures can be used with a wider cuff, this does not necessarily equate to a safer stimulus". Until you know the width of each cuff you cannot place it in a class or compare it with the figures above. The course page gives no width and the Amazon listing gives none in centimetres, so that figure has to come from the seller.

Does the material matter?

Buckner et al. (2017) compared inflatable nylon and elastic cuffs of similar width on the upper arm and found "Resting arterial occlusion pressure was different between the nylon [139 (14) mmHg] and elastic [246 (71) mmHg, p < 0.001] cuffs." Once each cuff was inflated to a pressure relative to its own occlusion pressure, "there were no differences in repetitions to failure, torque, muscle thickness, or muscle activation between the cuffs." Patterson et al. (2019) summarise the literature the same way: "the material of the cuff does not appear to impact the outcomes of BFR-RE." The Amazon listing names its materials, and the course page's silence on them is a smaller gap than the silence on width.

Limits of this evidence

None of these papers tested the BFR Pro 2.0. Loenneke et al. (2012) and Loenneke et al. (2013) measured occlusion at rest on the leg in healthy adults; Buckner et al. (2017) measured acute responses in the arm, not growth over weeks; Patterson et al. (2019) is a review and guidelines paper, and one of its authors is affiliated with a private company that runs BFR courses. The evidence supports the category, inflatable cuffs set by AOP, not this brand's bladder, gauge accuracy or sizing.

Who is it for, and who should skip it?

It fits

Someone who will follow the published protocol rather than a guessed setting. The Patterson et al. (2019) guideline table gives a load of 20 to 40% 1RM, 2 to 4 sets, 75 repetitions as 30, 15, 15, 15 or sets to failure, pressure at 40 to 80% AOP, 30 to 60 s rest between sets, a restriction time of 5 to 10 min per exercise with reperfusion between exercises, and a frequency of 2 to 3 times a week for more than 3 weeks (one of two frequency options in the table; the other is 1 to 2 times per day for 1 to 3 weeks). That needs AOP measured for each cuff on each limb (we advise a trained person does it; the guideline paper does not say who), and a cuff that holds pressure for the full restriction window. A full set suits someone training both arms and legs.

Skip it, or get clearance first

Patterson et al. (2019) report that "the totality of the literature reveals minimal adverse events pertaining to VTE and clinically reported events have not been reported." That is an average across studies, not a clearance for you. The same paper lists the VTE risk factors; anyone with one should get a qualified clinician's clearance before buying any cuff:

  • Medical conditions: "major orthopedic surgery, major general surgery, lower extremity paralysis due to spinal cord injury, pelvic, hip or long bone fractures, poly-trauma and cancer".
  • Additional risk factors: "a history of prior VTE, obesity, immobility, oral contraceptives, family history of VTE, physical inactivity and genetic conditions that affect blood clotting".
  • "Pregnancy carries an elevated risk both in the perinatal and post-natal periods".

Patterson et al. (2019) also note that cases of exertional rhabdomyolysis "are typically associated with an exercise load that greatly exceeds the fitness and normal physical exertion of the participant". Skip the set too if you will not measure AOP: a gauge set by feel is an expensive strap.

Frequently asked questions

Does the BFR Pro 2.0 state the cuff widths?+

No. The course page states no width, and the Amazon listing, read November 3, 2026, gives none in centimetres, only "Tailored to the optimal widths for upper & lower body training". Why does the cuff need a gauge at all?+

The published guidance sets pressure as a fraction of your own arterial occlusion pressure, between 40 and 80% of it, measured with the cuff you train with. That guidance is built on pneumatic cuffs, where pressure can be read and set; a strap or elastic wrap without a gauge gives no such reading. An inflatable cuff with a readable gauge fits that method.

Can I use a pressure number from a study with these cuffs?+

No. Occlusion pressure changes with cuff width, material and the limb. In one leg dataset of 83 people with a narrow 5 cm cuff, a fixed 200 mmHg would have put 19 of them at or above full arterial occlusion. Have a trained person measure your occlusion pressure with your own cuff on the limb you will train, then set a fraction of that reading.
