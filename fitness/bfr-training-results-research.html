BFR Training Results: What the Research Shows (2026)

By the Purpose with Presence Fitness team · Updated November 6, 2026 · 10 min read

Quick answer: In the meta-analyses we read, light-load blood flow restriction beat the same loads without it and matched heavy lifting on muscle size, while heavy lifting led on strength and adding BFR to it showed no added benefit. Certainty is very low to low where graded (Mckee, Pavlou); group averages are not individual forecasts.

What are BFR results measured against?

BFR training results only mean something next to a comparison, and the literature uses three. The first is low load without BFR: the same light lifting with no cuff. The second is heavy lifting. Lixandrão (2018) defined low-load BFR as under 50% of one-repetition maximum and conventional high-load training as above 65% 1RM. The third is heavy lifting with and without BFR added.

Most reviews report standardized effect sizes (SMD, g or ES), a difference between groups in standard deviations. A confidence interval that crosses zero is compatible with no difference. We read the abstracts, not the full text, of most sources and say where an abstract leaves a gap.

How does BFR compare with the same light load without BFR?

This is the comparison where BFR looks best. The umbrella review by Oborn (2025) pooled 47 meta-analyses comprised of 265 unique studies across all populations. It found that BFR exercise had a small effect on hypertrophy (SMD = 0.39, p < 0.001) and a moderate effect on strength (SMD = 0.61, p < 0.001) compared with low load. The authors rated all reviews as low-moderate quality, which is a review-quality rating, not a certainty grade.

In healthy adults older than 60, Fabero-Garrido (2022) pooled 14 studies. Compared with traditional low-load training, low-load BFR produced small to moderate increases in strength (seven studies; SMD 0.44 [0.28; 0.60]) and hypertrophy (two studies; SMD 0.51 [0.06; 0.96]). The hypertrophy figure rests on two studies with a wide interval, so its size is uncertain.

In clinical musculoskeletal rehabilitation, Hughes (2017) pooled 20 studies: ACL reconstruction (n=3), knee osteoarthritis (n=3), older adults at risk of sarcopenia (n=13) and sporadic inclusion body myositis (n=1). The authors concluded that compared with low-load training, low-load BFR training is more effective and tolerable, and a potential clinical rehabilitation tool.

How does BFR compare with heavy lifting?

Against heavy lifting, the pattern is similar muscle size and lower strength. Lixandrão (2018) reported higher strength increases for high-load training than for BFR training, and similar hypertrophic effects between the two. They concluded that maximum strength may be optimized by high-load training, while both approaches seem equally effective for muscle mass.

Oborn (2025) agrees on direction: BFR had its small and moderate effects compared with low load "but not high load", and its effects on hypertrophy and strength are smaller than traditional high-load training. Against high-load training the umbrella review reported hypertrophy SMD = -0.13 (p = 0.142) and strength SMD = -0.28 (p < 0.001). In older individuals, Centner (2019) pooled 11 studies with N = 238 and found that low-load BFR promoted similar hypertrophy to high-load training (ES 0.21, 95% CI -0.14 to 0.56) but lower strength gains (ES -0.42, 95% CI -0.70 to -0.14).

The over-60 review by Fabero-Garrido (2022) points the same way. Heavy lifting produced a small strength increase over low-load BFR (eight studies; SMD -0.23 [-0.41; -0.05]) but no detectable hypertrophy difference (six studies; SMD 0.08 [-0.22; 0.38]). No detectable difference is not proof of equality.

For clinical groups, Hughes (2017) reported a pooled strength effect of g=0.523 (95% CI 0.263 to 0.784) for low-load BFR and g=0.674 (95% CI 0.296 to 1.052) for heavy-load training, and described BFR as less effective than heavy-load training. The abstract does not state the comparison arm for each figure, so the two numbers are not a direct head-to-head.

Does adding BFR to heavy lifting help?

The most recent healthy-adult review says no, with a caveat. Mckee (2026) included eight chronic and 34 acute studies; very-low certainty evidence from the chronic studies suggests that adding BFR to high-load training does not further improve strength (g = 0.11, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.33) or size (g = 0.08, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.34). Very low certainty means a later study could change this. The authors wrote that current evidence suggests adding BFR to high-load training is not beneficial for enhancing muscle size and strength.

The practical reading, which is ours and not a finding: the reviews describe BFR as of particular interest for people with physical limitations that make heavy lifting hard, and the very-low-certainty evidence found no added benefit from adding it to heavy lifting. The guidance paper by Patterson (2019) describes the method with loads of 20 to 40% 1RM.

Do results differ by population?

Healthy adults

Mckee (2026) and Oborn (2025) are the broadest sources here. Together they show a gain over light loads alone, a smaller gain than heavy lifting, and no gain from adding BFR to heavy lifting, the last at very low certainty.

Population not stated

Slysz (2016), whose abstract does not name a population, pooled 19 studies (400 participants for strength, 377 for muscle size) and reported a mean strength improvement of 0.3kg for BFR resistance exercise and a mean muscle-size difference of 0.4cm(2) between experimental and control groups.

Adults over 60

The over-60 figures from Fabero-Garrido (2022) and Centner (2019) are above. Both rest on small groups of studies.

Clinical musculoskeletal rehabilitation

The Hughes (2017) pooled data cover ACL reconstruction, knee osteoarthritis, sarcopenia risk and one inclusion body myositis study. Safety in this group was reviewed by Minniti (2020), who included nineteen studies with a pooled sample size of 322. Three studies reported rare adverse events, including an upper extremity deep vein thrombosis and rhabdomyolysis, and the authors concluded that BFR appears to be a safe strengthening approach for knee-related musculoskeletal disorders but that further research is needed, including on who is at risk for rare events.

Upper body

Pavlou (2023) searched 346 articles and found 9 eligible studies on upper-body BFR and muscles proximal to the cuff. The evidence for all outcomes was of very low to low certainty. There were no significant differences between low-load BFR and heavy lifting in strength, and the authors judged low-load BFR as possibly equally effective for strength with low certainty. The authors report significant differences favoring low-load BFR over low-load training for bench press and shoulder flexion strength, and for shoulder lean mass and pectoralis major thickness against low-load and heavy training respectively, yet conclude with low certainty that size adaptations are not superior to either. The abstract's results and conclusion do not line up cleanly.

How long until BFR results show up?

The review abstracts we read do not give a time to a measurable result, so we do not offer one. The only timing in our sources is a training guideline: Patterson (2019) lists a frequency of 2 to 3 times a week for more than 3 weeks, or 1 to 2 times per day for 1 to 3 weeks.

What limits these results?

Four limits recur. First, quality: Oborn (2025) rated every included review low-moderate. Second, certainty: Pavlou (2023) graded evidence very low to low, and Mckee graded its finding very low. Third, small evidence bases, such as one Fabero-Garrido hypertrophy estimate resting on two studies. Fourth, methods differ: Patterson (2019) notes a wide range of pressures used in practice, and cuff width and load also vary, which makes pooled numbers an average over unlike protocols.

Safety is a separate question from effectiveness. Patterson (2019) states that many published trials do not directly measure for venous thromboembolism, and that the literature reveals minimal VTE adverse events and no clinically reported events. It also lists risk factors: major orthopedic or general surgery, lower extremity paralysis due to spinal cord injury, pelvic, hip or long bone fractures, poly-trauma and cancer, plus a history of prior VTE, obesity, immobility, oral contraceptives, family history of VTE, physical inactivity and genetic conditions that affect blood clotting. Pregnancy carries an elevated risk too. That is a general clotting-risk list, not a BFR clearance checklist. If any of it applies to you, get clearance from a doctor first.

The NHS guidance for adults aged 19 to 64 says to speak to your GP first if you have not exercised for some time, or if you have medical conditions or concerns. Our contraindications guide goes through who should not use the method.

Frequently asked questions

How long until BFR training results show up?+

The reviews we read do not report a time to a measurable change, so we cannot give one. The only timing in our sources is a guideline for how studies scheduled sessions: 2 to 3 times a week for more than 3 weeks. Individual response varies, and pooled averages hide that spread. Track your own training log rather than a deadline.

Does BFR build strength as well as heavy lifting?+

Not in the reviews we read. Against heavy lifting, low-load BFR gave similar muscle size gains but lower strength gains in the pooled data for older adults and in a broader comparison. Heavy lifting led on strength. The reviews describe BFR as of particular interest when heavy loads are hard to use, such as during some rehabilitation.

Does BFR work for older adults?+

Reviews of adults over 60 report that low-load BFR beat low-load training without BFR for strength and hypertrophy, and matched heavy lifting for hypertrophy but not strength. The evidence base is small, one hypertrophy estimate rests on two studies, and the authors call older-adult evidence sparse. Medical clearance matters before starting.

Does BFR work for rehabilitation?+

A review of clinical musculoskeletal groups, including ACL reconstruction and knee osteoarthritis, concluded that low-load BFR is more effective and tolerable than low-load training without it and is a potential rehabilitation tool. It was less effective than heavy-load training. Use it only under the supervision of the clinician managing the injury.

Does adding BFR to heavy lifting give extra gains?+

In the most recent healthy-adult meta-analysis we read, no, at very low certainty. Across eight chronic studies, adding BFR to high-load training did not further improve strength (g = 0.11, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.33) or size (g = 0.08, 95% CI -0.18 to 0.34), and both intervals crossed zero. Very low certainty means a later study could change this.

Are the effect sizes a promise of what I will gain?+

No. Effect sizes are pooled averages across study groups, measured in standard deviations. Where certainty was graded it was very low to low, and the umbrella review rated its included reviews low-moderate quality. They show direction and rough size for groups under study conditions. They cannot forecast what one person will gain from a given program.
