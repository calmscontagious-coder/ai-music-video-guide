The streak lesson

One small lesson a day, a counter that goes up when you show up, and tomorrow’s lesson already in view. It is the most copied loop in learning apps, and the easiest one to build badly.

The loop

  1. promptYou open it, because you decided to
  2. actionA two-minute lesson with a visible end
  3. rewardTwo new things you can do, and the count goes up by one
  4. returnTomorrow's lesson is already named on the home screen

A streak lesson is three parts that depend on each other. The lesson is small enough that “I have no time” is not true. The streak turns a row of small days into one number you can see. The next step is shown, so coming back needs no decision.

Take away the lesson and you have a counter that counts nothing. Take away the counter and day nine feels the same as day one. Take away the next step and people finish a lesson, feel good, and never think of it again.

Why it holds attention

Three mechanisms, each with evidence behind it:

  • A chain you do not want to break. A study of seven experiments found that an unbroken streak raises the chance someone does the activity again, and that a break discourages people most when they blame themselves and least when the streak can be repaired (Silverman and Barasch, Journal of Consumer Research, 2023).
  • Speeding up near the finish. People put in more effort as a goal gets closer (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, 2006). “Lesson 4 of 13” does this work for you. An endless course does not.
  • Ability. A behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt arrive together (the Fogg Behavior Model). You cannot control motivation. You can make the lesson two minutes long.

The largest language-learning app publishes how much this loop matters to it: its former product lead wrote that the share of daily users on a streak of seven days or more nearly tripled, to over half, in the years its growth returned. The same essay warns that people with long streaks were always going to be the keen ones, so do not read a streak as the cause of everything.

The version you would defend

A streak can be a kindness or a threat. The difference is what happens on the day someone misses.

Build these in from the first commit:

  1. The lesson ends. After today’s lesson the home screen says so and offers nothing that counts. Practice is there for people who want it, and it is labelled “does not count”.
  2. A missed day is forgiven. Dit gives two rest days. One missed day spends a rest day instead of the streak. One comes back every seven days.
  3. A broken streak takes nothing else away. The message is “the streak starts again today; the 6 letters you know are still yours.” What was learned is the real asset. The number is a way of seeing it.
  4. No reminders until asked for. The starter sends none. If you add them, the person turns them on, picks the time, and one tap turns them off.
  5. Leaving is easy. Copy your data, erase your data, both on the first settings screen.

Not taught here

Streak-loss shaming (“You let your streak die”), a paid streak repair offered at the moment of loss, a sad mascot, a notification that pretends to be a person, and a countdown to midnight. Each of these works by making someone feel bad about a day they were busy. This guide does not teach them.

Run the regret test on your own build: if the person could see everything you know about why each screen is there, would they still do the lesson? For a two-minute lesson with a visible end, yes.

Data model

One record, kept on the device.

Field Meaning
streak, best current and longest run of days
lastDone the last day a lesson was finished, as YYYY-MM-DD
rests rest days in hand, 0 to 2
lesson index of the next lesson
known what has been learned so far
sound a preference

Dates are stored as calendar days in the person’s own time zone, not as timestamps. A streak is about days as that person lives them. Storing a timestamp and comparing against a server’s midnight is the most common streak bug there is.

Screens

  1. Home. The count, what the count means right now, today’s lesson, what you know. Empty state: “None yet. The first lesson takes about two minutes.”
  2. Lesson. One question at a time, a progress bar, “3 of 8”. A way out at the top.
  3. Done. What you gained, the streak, one button back. No upsell here.
  4. Settings. Sound, rest days explained, copy my data, erase my data.

Make it yours

Shipping Dit as it stands would be shipping this guide’s example. Change at least these five things:

  1. The subject. Replace CODE and LESSONS. Knots, chords, sign-language letters, wine regions, the Greek alphabet, your own course.
  2. The name and the mark. Not a pun on a well-known app’s name. Search both stores first.
  3. The look. New palette, new type, your own drawing. The lamp is Dit’s; find yours in your subject.
  4. One rule. Three lessons a week instead of daily. A shared streak for two people. A streak counted in weeks.
  5. One screen. A map of the course. A certificate at the end. A “teach it back” step.

Ship it

  • Where it sells. Education and self-improvement, as a subscription or a one-time purchase of the full course.
  • The honest version of the paywall. The first lessons free, the price shown in real money before any billing detail is asked for, and cancelling as easy as subscribing (the US law is ROSCA, 15 U.S.C. 8403).
  • The base rate. Across subscription apps the median app earns about $72 a month a year after launch, and about one in six reaches $1,000 a month within two years (RevenueCat, State of Subscription Apps 2026). A streak does not change those odds. A subject people already want to learn, and a way of reaching them, does.
  • First hundred users. Go where people already study your subject and give the first three lessons away as a link that works in a browser. That is why the starter is a web page.
