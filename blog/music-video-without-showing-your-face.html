How to Make a Music Video Without Showing Your Face: 7 Routes Ranked by Cost and Anonymity

By the AI Music Video Guide team · Updated August 3, 2026 · 6 min read

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: You can make a real music video without ever appearing on camera. The seven proven routes are: a masked or costumed shoot, hands-and-objects b-roll, hired actors or dancers, commissioned 2D/3D animation, a lyric video, a stock-footage edit, and AI-generated video. The cheapest fully anonymous options are a DIY lyric video (near $0) and AI video (from around $10/month as of July 2026). Only three routes — lyric videos, stock edits, and AI — require no camera at all, and only AI produces original moving footage without filming anything or paying a performer.

Why do so many successful artists hide their faces?

Facelessness is a strategy, not a handicap. Sia built one of the biggest pop careers of the last two decades while covering her face, telling interviewers she wanted control over her image and didn't want her appearance critiqued more than her music (source). Alan Walker has described his hoodie-and-mask look as both privacy protection and a statement of unity — anyone could be behind the mask (source). BoyWithUke went from bedroom TikTok uploads to Billboard-charting singles entirely behind an LED mask, only revealing his face by choice in October 2023 (source). And Gorillaz took it furthest: a chart-topping band whose public members are four animated characters created by Damon Albarn and Jamie Hewlett (source).

Whether your reason is a day job, stage fright, safety, or a deliberate mystique play, the precedent is solid. If you're building the anonymous identity itself — name, character, visual world — start with our virtual artist branding guide, then come back here to pick the video route that fits.

Route 1: The mask or costume shoot

This is the Alan Walker play — you're on camera, but your face isn't. It keeps real human performance in the video, which no other route on this list fully replicates. The honest catch: it's still a full production. You need a camera operator (or a very patient tripod workflow), lighting, a location, and editing. Typical professional indie music video budgets run roughly $1,500–$4,000 (source), and a masked shoot sits in the same range — climbing further with crew size, locations, and shoot days. Anonymity is also only partial: your build, movement, tattoos, and location details are all visible, which matters if your goal is genuine privacy rather than aesthetic mystery.

Route 2: Hands-and-objects b-roll

Film everything except your face: fingers on frets, keys, and faders; vinyl spinning; pedalboards; rain on windows; your neighborhood at night. A phone, a cheap tripod, and a shot list get you a surprisingly intimate performance video for close to nothing. The trade-off is effort and ceiling — you're the camera department, and the format suits moody singer-songwriter material better than high-energy tracks. It's the best free option if you actually play instruments and want the video to prove it.

Route 3: Hired actors or dancers

Total anonymity for you, real humans on screen — someone else carries the narrative. Budget honestly: Dancers Alliance advises members not to accept non-union music video shoot days below $500 per dancer (source), and non-union background actors typically run $100–$200 per day (source) — before you add a director, camera, and editor. Realistic all-in cost for a small narrative video starts around $1,500–$4,000 and climbs quickly as you add crew, locations, and shoot days. This is the strongest route when you have a story concept and some budget, and the weakest when you have neither.

Route 4: Commissioned 2D/3D animation

The Gorillaz route, and the most premium-feeling anonymous format. It's also the slowest and most expensive: a fully animated three-minute 2D video typically runs $15,000–$70,000 from a studio, with basic 2D work from smaller studios starting around $10,000–$15,000 for a simple video (source). Timelines are measured in weeks to months, not days. If animation is your long-term brand, it's worth it; if you need a video for a release next month, it usually isn't.

Route 5: The lyric or kinetic-type video

The cheapest legitimate music video that exists. Hiring it out costs roughly $50–$1,000 depending on complexity (source), and DIY template tools get you there for free or nearly free. Platforms treat lyric videos as real releases, fans genuinely use them to learn songs, and your face never enters the equation. We walk through the full DIY process in how to make a lyric video with AI — for most artists this should be the default first video, not a consolation prize.

Route 6: The stock-footage edit

Cut royalty-free clips to your track. Libraries like Pexels license footage free for commercial use with no attribution required (source), so your only costs are editing software and time. The honest weaknesses: the footage wasn't shot for your song, other artists are pulling from the same pool, and making unrelated clips feel intentional takes real editing skill. It works best for ambient, electronic, and instrumental music where mood matters more than narrative.

Route 7: AI-generated video

This is the only route where no camera, crew, or on-screen human exists anywhere in the chain — you describe scenes in text, and the model generates original footage. Which routes still require filming something or paying someone?

Be clear-eyed before committing. Masked shoots and hands-and-objects b-roll still need a camera and a shoot day. Hired performers need a crew and $1,500+ in realistic budget. Commissioned animation needs no camera but demands weeks and a five-figure spend for studio quality. Only three routes are pure desk work: lyric videos, stock edits, and AI generation. That's not an argument that desk routes are automatically better — a great masked performance video beats a mediocre AI video — but it is the honest map of what each choice costs you in money, time, and other people.

Frequently asked questions

Can you have a successful music career without ever showing your face?+

Yes — there's decades of precedent. Sia topped charts while covering her face with wigs, Alan Walker built a global EDM career behind a mask and hoodie, Gorillaz became a chart-topping band represented entirely by animated characters, and BoyWithUke reached the Billboard charts from behind an LED mask before choosing to unmask in 2023. Platforms, playlists, and fans judge the music and the visual world around it — not whether the artist's face appears in it.

What is the cheapest way to make a music video without being on camera?+

A DIY lyric video is the cheapest — free template tools or a $50–$150 freelancer get you a real, releasable video. Next cheapest are stock-footage edits (libraries like Pexels license clips free for commercial use) and AI-generated video, which as of July 2026 starts around $10/month for tools that turn text prompts into original footage. All three require no camera, crew, or performer.

Do AI music video tools require any filming at all?+

No. AI video tools generate original footage from text descriptions and reference images — no camera, crew, or on-screen human exists anywhere in the process. As of July 2026, Seedance 2.0 generates clips up to 15 seconds each, chains them into longer sequences with video extension, and accepts your finished song as an audio reference. The trade-off is a learning curve: you direct with written prompts instead of a camera, and keeping characters consistent across scenes takes deliberate technique.

Does wearing a mask in a music video actually keep me anonymous?+

Only partially. A mask hides your face, but your height, build, movement, tattoos, voice, and shooting locations all remain visible and identifiable to people who know you. If your goal is aesthetic mystery — like Alan Walker's branded mask — that's fine. If your goal is genuine privacy from coworkers or your community, fully faceless formats like animation, lyric videos, stock edits, or AI-generated visuals hide you far more completely.

How much does an animated music video cost to commission?+

A fully animated three-minute 2D music video from a studio typically costs $15,000–$70,000, with 3D starting around $30,000. At the budget end, basic 2D work from smaller studios starts around $10,000–$15,000 for a simple video. Timelines run weeks to months. It's the most premium anonymous format — the Gorillaz model — but only makes sense if animation is your long-term brand identity, not if you need a video for next month's release.

Is stock footage legal to use in a music video I'll monetize?+

Yes, if the license allows commercial use. Pexels, for example, licenses its photos and videos free for personal and commercial projects with no attribution required — though you can't resell unaltered clips or imply endorsement by people shown in them. Always read the specific license of each library you pull from, and keep a record of where each clip came from in case a platform's content system ever flags your video.
