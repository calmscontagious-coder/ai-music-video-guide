How to Make a Music Video With Seedance in 2026 (Full Walkthrough)

If you want to know how to make a music video with Seedance, the honest short answer is that you don't render one long video. You build it out of a dozen or more short clips, feed the platform your audio so the picture moves with the beat, and reuse the same reference image so one performer shows up in every shot. We make AI music videos daily and have run this exact pipeline, so this walkthrough is the version with the real steps, the credit math, and the limits stated plainly.

Seedance (the platform at seeddance.video) is worth a musician's attention for one specific reason: it bundles 30+ generative engines behind a single subscription and one shared credit pool, and it lets you upload an audio track so the motion locks to your song (Source: seeddance.video). That combination is what separates this from the general how to make an AI music video from a song process, where you usually stitch three or four separate tools together.

What is Seedance, and why do musicians use it?

Seeddance (seeddance.video) is an independent subscription platform, not ByteDance, that bundles what it calls 30+ engines (Seedance 2.0 and 2.5, Veo 3, image models such as Nano Banana and SeeDream 4, plus Suno for music) behind one subscription and a shared credit pool (Source: seeddance.video/pricing, read October 25, 2026). Its footer still lists Sora 2, but OpenAI discontinued the Sora web app and mobile app on April 26, 2026 and the Sora API on September 24, 2026 (Source: OpenAI Help Center, read October 25, 2026), so don't plan a video around it. Musicians use it because it accepts an uploaded audio track, so clips render in time with the song instead of being cut to it afterward.

So in theory you can write the track and shoot the video without leaving the account. If you're weighing the underlying models against each other first, our Sora 2 vs Veo 3 vs Seedance breakdown covers where each one wins.

The Seedance models are the ones that matter for music video work. They're multimodal, and the reference limits are set per type rather than as one combined count. Seedance 2.0 takes up to 9 images, 3 video clips and 3 audio files in MP3 or WAV, each audio file 2 to 15 seconds and 15 seconds combined (Source: Morphic Seedance 2.0 guide, July 2026). Seedance 2.5 raises that to 30 images, 10 videos and 10 audio clips (Source: ByteDance Seed, July 31, 2026). Either way you reference the files through an @-tag prompt grammar, so one prompt can point at your character image and an excerpt of your track (Source: WaveSpeed). It also emits video and a synchronized audio mix in one inference pass, which is what lets the picture lock to a rhythm rather than get cut to it afterward.

Step 1: Build a shot list from your song's structure

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that decides whether the video looks intentional or like a slideshow. A single Seedance render is short, and the ceiling depends on the version. Seedance 2.5 renders 4 to 30 seconds in one pass (Source: Flick, July 2026; seeddance.video Seedance 2.5 page, read October 25, 2026), double Seedance 2.0's 15 seconds (Source: ByteDance Seed, July 31, 2026). So a 3-minute (180-second) video is assembled from anywhere between 6 clips (all 30-second takes) and 45 (all 4-second shots). You need a plan before you spend a credit.

Map your clips to the song's arrangement. Open the track and mark the sections, then assign shots:

  • Intro — one establishing shot, wide, sets location and mood.
  • Verse — 2 to 4 shots of your character/performer, closer framing, less motion.
  • Chorus — your biggest shots: movement, scale, the visual hook that repeats. Reuse the same chorus look each time it comes back.
  • Bridge — a contrast: new location, different palette, a break in the pattern.
  • Outro — one shot that resolves, mirroring the intro.

Write a one-line prompt per shot before you generate anything. Doing the thinking on paper is free; doing it inside the render queue costs credits.

Step 2: Upload your track so Seedance beat-syncs the motion

This is the part no general video tool does. Seedance lets you upload an audio sample to lock tempo and mood, so clips are generated in time with the music instead of edited to it later (Source: Opus). In the @-tag grammar you reference the file — something like @Audio1 as the backing track — and the model beat-locks the cut to that MP3 (Source: WaveSpeed).

Practical note from our runs: upload the specific slice of audio that matches the shot, not the whole song, since each render only covers a few seconds. Feed the chorus stem to the chorus clips and the verse stem to the verse clips. Because each render is short, you can iterate shot by shot in one sitting, regenerating any clip that misses.

Step 3: Lock character consistency across every shot

The failure mode of AI music videos is that the singer's face changes every clip. Seedance solves this the same way it handles audio: with reference images. Supply the same reference images to each render through the @-tag system and the same performer recurs across all your short clips (Source: WaveSpeed). Seedance 2.0 allows 9 images, but for a face fewer works better: one face-only headshot plus one full-body shot for costume and proportion. Morphic reports that a multi-angle character sheet makes the model read several different people (Source: Morphic, July 2026).

Our approach: generate or choose one strong character image first, then paste that same @image reference into every shot prompt, changing only the action and setting. If your character wears a specific jacket or has a specific hair color, keep that image constant across the whole shot list. Consistency comes from feeding the model the same anchor, not from describing the person in words each time.

How many credits does a Seedance music video cost?

On seeddance.video, Seedance 2.5 costs 12 credits per second at 480p, 23 at 720p and 48 at 1080p (Source: seeddance.video Seedance 2.5 page, read October 25, 2026). A 3-minute (180-second) video is therefore about 2,160 credits at 480p or 4,140 at 720p. Add 20 to 40 percent for re-rolled shots. At the annual Basic plan’s $0.066-per-credit rate, the 720p render is about $273 in raw credits before re-rolls (Source: seeddance.video/pricing, read October 25, 2026).

Credits are consumed per second of generated video, and the rate depends on the model and the resolution. Seedance 2.5 offers 480p, 720p and 1080p; Seedance 2.0 costs more per second, 20 credits at 480p and 40 at 720p (Source: seeddance.video Seedance 2.0 page, read October 25, 2026). The platform's separate Music to Video tool, which takes a whole song up to 10 minutes and doesn't name the model behind it, charges 2 credits a second at 480p and 4 at 720p (Source: seeddance.video Music to Video page, read October 25, 2026), so a 3-minute song there is 360 or 720 credits. It is the cheaper route when you don't need shot-by-shot control over references. The exact credit cost of any given render is shown before you commit, so you're never guessing blind.

Free vs paid: what you can actually release

The tiers are where people get burned, so read them before you pay. The free tier is 1 credit every 24 hours, claimed after sign-in (Source: seeddance.video/pricing, read October 25, 2026). The shortest Seedance 2.5 render, 4 seconds at 480p, costs 48 credits, so the free credit lets you look around the interface but not render a usable shot.

Watermark-free MP4 and PNG exports and a commercial license covering your outputs come with the paid plans only, not the free tier (Source: seeddance.video). If you plan to release the video publicly or monetize it, that distinction matters. The cheapest paid plan is Basic at $9.90 a month on annual billing (1,800 credits issued upfront, $0.066 per credit) or $19.90 month to month (200 credits a month). Pro is $19.90 a month on annual billing (4,200 credits upfront, $0.057 per credit) or $39.90 month to month (500 credits a month) (Source: seeddance.video/pricing, read October 25, 2026). Match the tier to your shot count: a full 720p Seedance 2.5 video at about 4,140 credits plus re-rolls uses roughly a year of annual Pro credits, so do the math on your shot list before you pick.

Once your clips are rendered, assemble them in any editor — order by the shot list, trim to the beats, and add crossfades where sections change. The generation is the hard part; the edit is straightforward.
