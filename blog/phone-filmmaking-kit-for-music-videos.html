The Phone Filmmaking Kit for Music Videos: What to Buy First

By the AI Music Video GuideUpdated November 8, 20269 min read

how this page earns

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: Buy a wireless mic first, then a tripod, then a small light. Those three fix the problems that make a phone music video look amateur: thin sound, shaky framing and a flat face. Add a gimbal when you shoot moving performance shots, an ND filter for outdoor sun, and a cage, brighter light and SSD last.

A phone filmmaking kit for music videos is not one purchase. It's a stack, and the order matters more than the brands. This guide covers what each piece changes in the finished video, what to buy first when you can only buy one thing, and where iPhone and Android split. It sits alongside our studio gear list and our walkthrough of making an AI music video on your phone.

What should you buy first for a music video shot on a phone?

A wireless microphone, because viewers forgive soft video long before they forgive bad audio, and a phone's own mic picks up the room, the wind and your hands. Second, a tripod, so locked-off performance shots stop drifting. Third, a small bi-color light, which separates a face from a dark bedroom wall. Everything after that is refinement.

We did not lab-test these products ourselves; this guide combines maker specifications with what independent reviewers report, with sources linked. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

Tier 1, the mic: DJI Mic Mini (paid link)

Even when the final track is a studio recording, you need clean on-set sound to sync lips and keep any live vocal. The transmitter weighs about 10 g and is rated for approximately 11.5 hours; DJI's 400 m range figure was measured in an open space free of interference, so plan on far less in a city (DJI). The link above is the two-transmitter set with the charging case. We covered it in more depth on our dedicated mic page.

The weaknesses are real. The transmitters lack internal and 32-bit float recording as safety nets, and noise cancelling in a busy city still lets a passing siren through (SoundGuys). If a take matters, record a backup on the phone.

The other sane pick is the Rode Wireless Micro (paid link). It plugs straight into the phone, arrives paired, and Rode quotes up to 21 hours of battery with the charging case (Rode). Buy the USB-C or Lightning version to match your phone; the receiver-free Bluetooth mode works only on Apple phones through Rode's own app (Rode). Downsides: no physical controls, windscreens that don't fit in the case, and no onboard backup recording (SoundGuys; The Podcast Host).

Tier 1, the tripod: Joby GorillaPod 1K Gen 2 (paid link) or Ulanzi MT-44 (paid link)

A locked-off wide shot is the backbone of a performance video, and a tripod turns a drifting handheld take into something you can cut to on the beat. The GorillaPod's legs wrap around railings and branches, it carries a 1 kg load at 315 g, and its magnetic phone mount sits beside a quarter-inch thread so a light or a second camera can share it; a magnetic adapter ring comes in the box (Joby).

If you want height for a full-body shot, the MT-44 extends to 146.5cm, hides its phone clamp inside the handle, and takes small cameras and lights as well (Ulanzi). Neither is a heavy video tripod; the bendy legs take practice to level and a tall thin pole catches wind.

Tier 1, the light: Ulanzi VL49 (paid link)

A small bi-color panel does one job in a music video: it puts a controlled key light on the face so the phone stops smearing it with noise and lifting the shadows into grey. Ulanzi lists 2500K to 9000K colour temperature, a 2000mAh battery rated for 2 to 10 hours depending on brightness, a magnetic back and a cold shoe underneath (Ulanzi). It also does saturated colours, which is handy for a quick neon look behind a singer.

Treat it as a fill or accent light: Ulanzi's own 2 to 10 hour figure means full power drains it quickly, and a 6W panel won't light a room (Ulanzi). The upgrade sits in tier 3.

Do you need a gimbal to shoot a music video on a phone?

Not for a static performance video, where a tripod wins. You need one the moment the camera moves with the artist: walking verses, orbit shots, handheld choruses. A gimbal smooths the walk, keeps the horizon level and, with tracking, follows the singer so you can shoot alone. After audio, it's the biggest jump in production value.

Tier 2, the gimbal: DJI Osmo Mobile 7P (paid link) or Insta360 Flow 2 Pro (paid link)

DJI's 7P weighs about 368 g with its built-in tripod, clamp and tracking module, takes phones from 170-300 g, and has a 215 mm extension rod (DJI). Its 3350 mAh battery is rated for approximately 10 hours, falling to approximately 5 hours with tracking on and approximately 4 hours with the fill light at maximum (DJI).

Check the listing says 7P: the plain Osmo Mobile 7 has no built-in extension rod and lists the module as optional rather than standard (DJI).

Reviewers say the module lets it track you in third-party camera apps, that it's the gimbal to buy for Android users, and that DJI's stabilisation is class-leading; the same review notes the arm can't turn a full circle and the module must be wired to the phone by USB-C (The Shortcut). One reviewer found the thicker clamp bulky and warned that the sealed battery sets the product's lifespan (Fstoppers).

The Flow 2 Pro weighs about 357 g, takes phones from 130 to 300g, has a 210mm selfie stick and runs 10 hours on a 1100mAh cell, a figure measured with no tracking module or fill light attached (Insta360). Insta360 says the same battery can charge your phone through the gimbal (Insta360). Check the listing says Flow 2 Pro, not the plain Flow 2.

Its trick is Apple's own subject tracking: on an iPhone it follows you in the native camera app and 200+ other iOS apps (Insta360).

That is why one reviewer calls it the smarter gimbal for iPhone users, while on Android its tracking works only inside Insta360's own app unless you add the separate AI Tracker (The Shortcut). Weaknesses: a plastic body, button markings that are hard to see and phone side buttons that are hard to reach once clamped (PCMag). It isn't waterproof either (Insta360).

Tier 2, the ND filter: Moment VND QuickLock (paid link) or Freewell iPhone VND/CPL kit (paid link)

Outdoors, a phone fights bright light by raising its shutter speed, which makes motion look choppy and video-game crisp. An ND filter cuts the light so the shutter can slow back down and motion blurs naturally, the thing most people mean by "cinematic" (Moment). Moment names it a 2 to 7 stop filter but its own spec table says 2 to 5; it uses built-in stops to prevent cross-polarisation, and only fits its own case on iPhone 15 and newer (Moment).

Freewell's kit moves through one to five stops and up to ten with its ND32 insert, again magnetically on its own iPhone case or cage (Freewell). Android users are worse served: both magnetic systems are built around iPhone cases, so the universal route is a clip-on holder or a cage with a filter thread, each with the trade-offs SmallRig lays out in its mounting guide (SmallRig).

Which pieces fit an iPhone and which fit Android?

Mics, tripods, lights and the SSD don't care which phone you own. The split is in tracking and filters. Apple's tracking standard favours the Flow 2 Pro on an iPhone; the Osmo Mobile 7P is the safer Android gimbal because its module tracks inside third-party apps. Magnetic filter systems are built around iPhone cases, so Android users go clip-on or cage.

Tier 3, the cage: SmallRig Universal Phone Cage (paid link)

A cage is how you stop juggling. It wraps the phone in a frame with quarter-inch threads and cold shoes, so the mic receiver, the light and a handle all ride on the phone, and it works horizontally or vertically with a case still on (SmallRig). SmallRig's own guide puts cage weight around 100 to 200 g, and that bulk is the trade: you lose the grab-and-shoot speed that made the phone appealing (SmallRig).

Tier 3, the better light: Aputure MC Pro (paid link)

When the small panel stops being enough, this is the lensed pocket panel to step up to. Aputure rates it from 2,000K to 10,000K at CRI 96, with a 5W maximum output (563 lumens), approximately 120 minutes at full power, 247g of weight, IP65 weather resistance and built-in magnets (Aputure).

An independent review credits the lensed optics for output near the best in class from 5W, and warns that the magnets can bother magnet-sensitive gear, the adhesive plates are single-use and the battery isn't user-replaceable (independent review).

Tier 3, the storage: Samsung T7 Shield (paid link)

Phone video eats storage and a music video shoot produces dozens of takes. Samsung's rugged drive reads at 1,050 MB/s and writes at 1,000 MB/s over USB 3.2 Gen2, weighs 98 grams, is IP65 rated, is drop resistant to three meters, works with Android phones and carries a three-year limited warranty; the link above is the 1TB version (Samsung).

TechGearLab measured about 676 MB per second sequential read on a Mac and 1061 MB per second on Windows, and the drive held those speeds after a 9.8-foot drop and a water spray; they list it as usable with Mac, PC, Android phones and consoles, with iPhone storage the exception, and note it comes in 1TB, 2TB and 4TB (TechGearLab).

Shutter Muse adds that makers' claimed speeds sit on the high side of reality, so treat the 1,050 figure as a ceiling (Shutter Muse).

Frequently asked questions

Can I shoot a whole music video with just a phone and a wireless mic?

Yes, and many people should start that way. A phone on a stable surface with a wireless mic gives you clean audio for sync and usable live vocals, which is the part viewers judge hardest. Framing and lighting can be solved with daylight, a window and a stack of books. Add gear only when a specific shot keeps failing.

Is a gimbal or a tripod more useful for a solo artist filming alone?

A tripod first. Solo performance videos are mostly locked-off shots, and a tripod makes those repeatable so you can film the same chorus from three angles and cut between them. A gimbal earns its place once you want the camera to move with you or follow you with tracking, which is harder to do alone without one.

Why does my outdoor phone footage look choppy, and will an ND filter fix it?

In bright sun the phone raises its shutter speed to avoid overexposure, and a very fast shutter freezes each frame so motion looks staccato. An ND filter is a neutral darkening layer that lets the phone use a slower shutter again, which brings back natural motion blur. It fixes that one problem well and does nothing for shaky hands or bad audio.

Do I need a phone cage if I already own a gimbal?

Usually not, and the two don't combine well because a caged phone is heavier and bulkier than most gimbal clamps accept. A cage suits tripod and handheld shoots where you want a mic receiver, a light and a handle attached to the phone at once. Pick the gimbal for moving shots and the cage for a static rig, not both on the same day.

How much storage does a phone music video shoot really need?

More than the phone has free, almost every time, because you will shoot many takes of each section at high quality. The practical fix is to offload to a portable SSD between locations rather than deleting takes on set. Check whether your phone can record straight to the drive or only copy to it, since that varies by model and operating system.
