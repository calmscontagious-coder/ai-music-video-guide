Suno vs Udio in 2026: Which One Should You Use Now?

By the AI Music Video Guide team · Updated October 21, 2026 · 12 min read

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: Use Suno if the song has to leave the app. As of October 2026 it is the only one of the two that exports files: Pro is $10 a month for 2,500 credits and 20 downloads, Premier $30 for 10,000 credits and 60. Udio has been streaming-only since October 29, 2025, and its licensed relaunch keeps it that way.

We make songs in both every week, and this Suno vs Udio comparison has changed shape twice since we first wrote it. In 2025 it was vocals against fidelity. By spring 2026 it was exports against no exports. Since September 2026 it is a third thing: Suno runs on a brand-new model family with hard download caps and a second major-label lawsuit, while Udio sits with 2025 models, no exports, and a fan app on the way you will not own anything inside.

Every price, date and version below comes from a page we opened on October 21, 2026.

What changed between Suno and Udio in 2026?

Suno replaced its entire model lineup with v6 on September 9, 2026 and started metering downloads on September 3. Udio kept its 2025 models and its download ban, and spent the year signing licenses for a fan-facing app. Both companies are still being sued by Sony. Suno is also being sued, twice, by Universal.

  • October 29, 2025: Universal settles with Udio; Udio disables downloads of audio, video and stems the same day (Source: UMG press release). A 48-hour download window follows on November 3 after a subscriber revolt (Source: Billboard, October 30, 2025).
  • November 2025: Warner settles with both Suno and Udio (Source: Forbes, December 18, 2025). Merlin, Kobalt and Believe license Udio by April 2026. Sony stays out.
  • June 5 and July 20, 2026: the musicians' union sues Universal, Atlantic and Warner Records over the Suno and Udio deals; Sony files a second suit against Udio over 30,117 recordings.
  • August 10, 2026: Suno announces download caps, new terms and the retirement of every existing model.
  • September 3 and 9, 2026: the caps go live; v6 launches and the older models go.
  • September 18, 2026: Universal and Sony sue Suno a second time, over 60,202 recordings.

What is Suno v6, and what happened to v4.5, v5 and v5.5?

v6 is a three-model family launched September 9, 2026 and built with Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe. Every earlier model was retired with the launch. Your old songs stay in your library and keep playing, but nothing new can be generated on them.

The models split by tier. v6 is the flagship for Pro and Premier, pitched as precise and consistent. v6-wild, also paid-only, is the exploratory one: less predictable, more textured. v6-mini is faster and the only model free accounts can use. New features are plain-language edits: change one section of a finished song, swap a single lyric, or start from an image or a video (Source: Suno, “Introducing v6”, September 9, 2026).

The retirement was announced on August 10, 2026, before anyone had heard v6: “When the new models launch, all prior models will be retired” (Source: Suno). Suno's FAQ adds the part that stings: extensions, remixes and covers of old songs now run on the new models, “so results may sound different from the original generation” (Source: Suno Help Center). There is no version pinning and no paid way back to v5.5.

What v6 was trained on is contested. At launch Suno said v6 was built “from scratch” on licensed catalog, user data and the team's learnings (Source: Music Ally, September 9, 2026). Answering the September lawsuit, it described that user data as “interactions including creations and preference signals,” which puts user-generated tracks inside the training set (Source: Music Business Worldwide, September 2026). The labels say those creations came out of the earlier, unlicensed models.

Reception has been rough. Complaints about flat, muddy vocals piled up on the Suno subreddit within days, and a hands-on reviewer heard less depth and body than v5.5 produced (Source: Gearnews, September 2026). Our runs land in the middle: the flagship follows a written brief more obediently than v5.5 did, and the vocal warmth we chased through 2025 is not fully back.

What did Udio's Universal settlement actually change?

It ended Universal's lawsuit, licensed Universal recordings and publishing to Udio, and switched off every download the same day. Subscribers got more credits in exchange. The money terms were never disclosed, and the product it set up is a fan remix app, not a new generator.

Udio's own help page lists the service changes: a one-time grant of 1,000 non-expiring credits, Standard's monthly limit raised from 1,200 to 2,400 credits, Pro's from 4,800 to 6,000. Then the line that matters: “downloading of audio, video, and stems has been disabled” (Source: Udio Help Center). Universal promised a new platform in 2026 (Source: UMG, October 29, 2025). Participating artists are paid for training and outputs and opt in tool by tool (Source: Billboard, October 2025).

The clearest picture came from a Udio and Kobalt webinar on April 30, 2026. The app will be called Starstruck: mobile-first, aimed at fans, with four modes (Cover, Reimagine, Remix, Create), every creation tied to a named artist and song, and the recording owned by that artist's rights holder, not the fan. Two subscription tiers are capped by monthly creations, and stream encryption, an inaudible watermark and distributor fingerprinting guard the garden (Source: Music Business Worldwide, May 2026). As of October 21, 2026 we can find no launch date, and udio.com still sells the legacy product.

How much do Suno and Udio cost in October 2026?

The headline prices are identical: $10 a month for the middle tier and $30 for the top tier, or $8 and $24 on annual billing. The difference is what the money buys. Suno's paid tiers include 20 or 60 downloads a month with commercial rights. Udio's include none.

The Suno cap has rules that soften it: one song is one download in any format, re-downloads do not count, and stems ride along. Unused downloads do not roll over, and the cap covers songs made before September 3, 2026 too (Source: Suno Help Center). An October 14, 2026 check found Suno still had not priced additional downloads (Source: Mubert, October 2026). Until it does, treat 20 or 60 as the ceiling. Which sounds better now, Suno v6 or Udio?

On raw fidelity Udio still wins for dense instrumentals, and its vocals carry more vibrato and breath than they get credit for. Suno v6 is more controllable and more consistent across a full song, but many users, and we, hear flatter vocals than v5.5 gave us.

A correction to our earlier version: Udio outputs 48 kHz stereo, and so do Suno's WAV exports; a September 2026 check of 25 recent Suno WAV files found 18 at 48 kHz (Source: Undetectr, September 25, 2026). Sample rate is not the separator; how the fidelity is spent is. Udio keeps cymbals, sibilance and string harmonics clear, and for ambient, cinematic and jazz beds it is still the better listen.

Udio's tooling is frozen. Its help center still lists the same v1, v1.5 and v1.5 Allegro models that predate the settlement (Source: Udio Help Center). Songs build in 32-second and 130-second chunks, and the Sessions editor from June 2025 arranges sections but does nothing with stems (Source: Undetectr, above). It is a sketchpad with excellent speakers.

Suno v6 is the opposite trade. Section edits by prompt, single-lyric swaps and a Studio with MIDI and automation on Premier make it the only one of the two you can finish a song inside. The cost, in our runs and in the subreddit threads, is a vocal that sits flatter than v5.5 did. None of this is lab-verified, and it will move as Suno patches v6. A lead vocal that has to sell a lyric starts in Suno for us every time; a dense instrumental reference starts in Udio and stays there; there is nowhere else it can go.

Can you download, own and release what you make?

On Suno, yes, inside the cap: paid subscribers are assigned Suno's rights in each song, and commercial use attaches to every permitted download and survives cancellation. On Udio, no: nothing can be downloaded, and on Starstruck the rights holder, not you, will own the result.

Suno's terms, in force since September 3, 2026, assign Pro and Premier subscribers “all of its right, title and interest” in songs made from their prompts, while adding that Suno “makes no representation or warranty to you that any copyright will vest in any Output.” Commercial use is tied to a permitted download, is “perpetual,” and survives cancellation or later cap changes. Free and trial downloads and remixes of other users' songs are personal use only, and stream-ripping or stripping a watermark is prohibited (Source: Suno Terms of Service, checked October 21, 2026). Suno said in August 2026 it plans to watermark songs (Source: TechCrunch, September 9, 2026).

Contractual ownership is not copyright; Suno cannot make a fully machine-generated recording copyrightable under US law. The hybrid approach still works: write your own lyrics, keep dated drafts, register the human parts. Which stores will take the result is covered in our list of platforms that accept AI music.

For music videos, a downloaded Suno master is the only thing you can hand to a video generator. Udio tracks cannot make that trip at all.

Where do the lawsuits stand in October 2026?

Suno faces two live Universal and Sony cases in Boston federal court, covering 560 and 60,202 recordings, with a theoretical ceiling above $9 billion on the second. Udio faces Sony alone, in New York, over 30,117 recordings. No court has ruled on fair use in either company's case.

On August 18, 2026 Judge F. Dennis Saylor IV refused to let the labels add 61,026 recordings to the 2024 Suno case, without prejudice, and let them add a YouTube stream-ripping claim (Source: Music Business Worldwide, September 2026). A month later the labels filed separately: 60,202 recordings at up to $150,000 per work, plus up to $2,500 per act of circumventing YouTube's download protection, roughly $150 million more. The complaint calls v6 “the fruit of the same poisoned tree” and argues that training on an infringing model's outputs “launders” the infringement (Source: Music Business Worldwide, September 2026). Suno called the claims “fundamentally flawed on both the facts and the law.”

On October 14, 2026 Universal and Sony asked a Manhattan judge to force DistroKid to produce month-by-month counts of AI-flagged uploads, their royalties, and how many trace to Suno. The same day they moved against Suno investors Bond Capital and Menlo Ventures; Bond led Suno's June 2026 Series D of more than $400 million at a $5.4 billion valuation (Source: Music Business Worldwide, October 2026). They want market-harm evidence because Suno's defense is fair use.

Elsewhere, on September 29, 2026 the Third Circuit rejected fair use for AI training in Thomson Reuters v. ROSS. Boston sits in the First Circuit, so the ruling is persuasive, not binding, and Suno can argue that no free corpus of recordings existed the way free judicial opinions did for ROSS (Source: Music Business Worldwide, October 2026). Suno also faces suits from musicians including David Lowery and Jason Isbell, Canada's SOCAN, and Round Hill Music (Source: Music Ally, above).

Udio's exposure is narrower and still real. Sony's second complaint, filed July 20, 2026 in the Southern District of New York, covers 30,117 recordings a judge had refused to add to the first case on June 29 (Source: MusicTech, July 22, 2026). The musicians' union suit over the licensing deals is pending too, and could reshape how settlement money flows (Source: AFM, August 2026). Our response has not changed: paid plan, download the master the day we make it, local archive. A library you cannot export is a queue.

Which one should you pick for each job?

Suno for anything that leaves the app, Udio only for listening-grade sketches, and Starstruck, when it ships, for fan remixes you never intend to own. The Suno vs Udio verdict is a logistics verdict, not a taste verdict.

Frequently asked questions

Can I download songs from Udio in 2026?+

No. Udio switched off downloads of audio, video and stems on October 29, 2025, the day it settled with Universal, and its help center still lists them as disabled in October 2026. The only exception was a 48-hour window in early November 2025. The coming Starstruck app is designed so creations never leave it, so exports are not expected back.

How many songs can I download from Suno each month?+

Pro allows 20 downloads a month and Premier 60, counted per song: re-downloads, extra formats and stems do not use another download. Unused downloads do not roll over. Free accounts opened before September 3, 2026 have up to 7 lifetime trial downloads with no commercial rights. Premier users working inside Suno Studio have no download cap. Extra downloads can be bought; Suno has not published the price.

Can I still use Suno v5 or v5.5?+

No. Suno retired every model before v6 when v6 launched on September 9, 2026, and nothing older appears in the model picker. Songs you already made stay in your library, playable and shareable, and you can still extend, cover or remix them, but those operations now run on v6, so the result can sound different from the original.

Do I own the music I make with Suno, and can I use it commercially?+

On Pro or Premier, Suno assigns you all of its rights in the song, and commercial use attaches to each permitted download and survives cancellation. Suno's terms also say it makes no warranty that copyright will vest in any output, so add human authorship: write the lyrics yourself and keep drafts. Free-tier songs and trial downloads are personal use only.

Is Udio worth paying for right now?+

Only as a listening tool. Standard at $10 and Pro at $30 a month buy 2,400 or 6,000 credits of high-fidelity generation you can play and share on Udio but never download. If you want to release music, the same money on Suno buys a real export path. If you want to remix major-label songs as a fan, wait for Starstruck.

Is Suno still being sued?+

Yes, twice by Universal and Sony in Boston federal court: the original 2024 case over 560 recordings and a second complaint filed September 18, 2026 over 60,202 recordings, which argues v6 was trained on the outputs of the earlier, unlicensed models. Warner settled in November 2025. Musicians, SOCAN and Round Hill have filed separate suits. No court has ruled on fair use yet.
