How to Fix AI Video Artifacts: Warped Hands, Melting Faces, Extra Fingers

By the AI Music Video Guide team · Updated July 31, 2026 · 6 min read

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: Most hand and face artifacts in AI video are cheaper to prevent than to fix. Keep hands out of frame or in simple poses, use medium shots instead of extreme close-ups, slow the motion down, and anchor identity with a reference image. Where the model supports it, add negative prompts: Kling has a dedicated negative-prompt box, Veo 3.1 accepts a negativePrompt API parameter, and Seedance takes an inline "no X, no Y" closing line in the main prompt. Reroll once before rewriting the prompt, and fix small glitches with a crop or cutaway in your editor. Some artifacts — legible on-screen text, complex finger-on-instrument contact — still are not reliably fixable at generation time in 2026, so plan your shot list around them.

If you are making a music video, you will hit these artifacts faster than most people, because performance footage is the worst case: fingers on fretboards, hands on keys, faces holding long emotional close-ups. Here is what actually works, what costs you money, and what to stop trying to fix.

Why does AI video warp hands and melt faces?

Hands are geometrically complex: fingers overlap, rotate, and interact with objects, and video models have to keep all of that consistent across dozens of frames. Faces fail differently — guides that analyze the problem describe temporal flickering (features shifting between frames), gradual "morphological drift" where the face slowly changes structure mid-shot, and unnatural expression blending (source). None of this is a bug you can toggle off. It is a limitation of how current models generate motion, which is why the fixes below are mostly about working around it rather than through it.

Which models support negative prompts — and what syntax works?

Negative prompts are the first thing most people reach for, and support varies more than you would expect. As of July 2026:

For hand and face problems specifically, a Kling prompting guide suggests negatives along the lines of "blur, distortion, morphing faces, extra limbs, unnatural physics" (source). In Seedance, our practice is a short closing line targeted at the symptom you actually saw: "- no warped face, no extra fingers, no deformed hands" after a bad hand render, or "- no text overlay, no watermark" when gibberish text appears.

Two honest caveats. First, negative prompts nudge the model; they do not guarantee anything. Second, stacking negatives backfires — past roughly six exclusions the model spends its effort rejecting things instead of rendering your scene (source). If the same artifact survives two negative-prompted attempts, the fix is not more negatives. It is a different shot.

How do you frame shots so artifacts never show up?

This is the highest-leverage fix on the page, and it costs nothing. Guidance from artifact-troubleshooting writeups is blunt: avoid hand close-ups because wide shots hide hand issues, frame shots to crop hands or keep them out of focus, use simple poses (fists and open palms render far better than detailed gestures), and cut away from faces during dynamic scenes (source). Slower motion and static or slow-moving cameras also hold consistency better than fast pans (source).

Translated to music video shots:

  • Guitar and piano close-ups are the single riskiest shot you can order. Shoot the solo as a silhouette, a wide shot, or an over-the-shoulder angle where fingers are motion-blurred or out of focus.
  • Vocalists holding a mic work well — a closed fist on a mic is exactly the "simple pose" models handle.
  • Medium shots beat extreme close-ups for faces, especially during belted choruses where the expression changes fast.
  • Keep camera moves deliberate. Slow push-ins and orbits fail less than whip pans. Our camera movement prompt guide covers phrasing that keeps motion controlled.

Does reference-image anchoring actually help?

Yes — it is the most reliable face fix available at generation time. Providing a clear character reference image anchors identity across a clip, and if a face looks right in frame one, you can feed that frame back as the reference for regeneration (source). Seedance accepts image references alongside your music track input, which is the standard workflow for keeping one artist recognizable across a multi-clip video. Reroll, re-prompt, or fix it in the edit — which is cheapest?

Every failed generation costs the same credits as a good one, so artifact handling is a budgeting decision. Our decision ladder:

  1. Reroll once with the identical prompt. A lot of artifacts are luck. One retry on the same prompt is the cheapest possible test.
  2. If the same artifact appears twice, re-prompt. Change the framing language ("medium shot," "hands resting at sides," simpler action) rather than adding more negatives. Two identical failures means the shot, not the seed, is the problem.
  3. If 80% of the clip is good, fix it in the edit. Punch in past the bad hand, trim the last second where the face drifts, or cut to B-roll over the glitch. An edit fix costs zero credits.

Can you repair artifacts after generation?

Partially. The reliable routes are ordinary editing: crop or punch in to reframe the artifact out, shorten the shot, cut away to B-roll, or slow the footage so a two-frame glitch disappears. These fix more real-world music videos than any AI tool does.

True video inpainting exists but is not yet a casual fix. Netflix open-sourced VOID in April 2026, a physics-aware video object-removal and inpainting model (source) released under Apache 2.0 (source) — impressive, but cleanup guides note it requires a high-VRAM GPU and comfort with open-source tooling (source). For most musicians, regenerating the shot is faster and cheaper than building an inpainting pipeline. And note what upscalers do not do: Topaz-style enhancement sharpens footage but does not correct anatomy — a warped hand in 4K is still a warped hand.

What still can't be fixed in 2026?

Three things, honestly:

  • Legible on-screen text. Models still produce gibberish signage and lettering often enough that you should never rely on generated text. Suppress accidental text with negatives, then add real titles and lyrics as overlays in your editor — that is how every serious AI lyric video is made.
  • Complex hand-object contact. Close-up fingerpicking, drumstick grips, piano runs shot from above. No prompt reliably fixes these; frame around them.
  • Long unbroken face close-ups. Identity drift compounds with duration. Cutting every few seconds resets it — which is also how extension chains work when you build clips past the per-generation limit. If your concept truly demands long single takes on a face, compare models first: our Kling vs Seedance comparison covers who holds up longer.

The uncomfortable truth is that the best artifact fix in 2026 is a shot list written by someone who knows the failure modes. Plan around hands, anchor faces with references, budget for retries, and let your editor absorb the rest.

Frequently asked questions

Why does AI video mess up hands so badly?+

Hands are geometrically complex: fingers overlap, rotate, and touch objects, and a video model must keep all of that consistent across dozens of frames. Faces fail for related reasons — features flicker between frames and slowly drift over long shots. It is a structural limitation of current models, not a settings problem, which is why framing around hands works better than prompting harder.

Does Seedance support negative prompts?+

Not as a dedicated field. As of July 2026, exclusions go inline at the end of your main prompt as a short closing line — for example "- no warped face, no extra fingers, no text on screen." Keep it to roughly three to six exclusions; stacking more tends to dull the whole generation. Kling is the major model with the clearest separate negative-prompt box, and Veo 3.1 accepts a negativePrompt parameter through its API.

Should I reroll or rewrite my prompt after a bad generation?+

Reroll once with the identical prompt first — many artifacts are seed luck, and one retry is the cheapest test. If the same artifact appears twice, rewrite the shot instead: simpler hand poses, a medium shot instead of a close-up, slower motion. If most of the clip is good, skip regeneration entirely and fix it in the edit with a crop, trim, or cutaway, which costs zero credits.

Can I fix a warped hand after the video is generated?+

Usually the practical fix is editorial: punch in to reframe the hand out, shorten the shot, or cut to B-roll over the glitch. True video inpainting exists — Netflix open-sourced its VOID model in April 2026 — but it needs a high-VRAM GPU and technical setup, so for most musicians regenerating the shot is faster and cheaper. Upscalers like Topaz sharpen footage but do not correct anatomy.

Will negative prompts fix gibberish text in my video?+

They help suppress accidental text — adding "no text overlay, no watermark, no subtitles" reduces random lettering showing up. But no 2026 model reliably generates legible on-screen text, so never prompt for real titles or lyrics. Generate clean footage and add text as overlays in your video editor instead; that is the standard workflow for AI lyric videos.
