Veo 3.1 Music Video Tutorial: From Storyboard to Beat-Cut Edit (July 2026)

By the AI Music Video Guide team · Updated July 30, 2026 · 7 min read

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: To make a music video with Veo 3.1, you storyboard your song into shots of 8 seconds or less, generate each shot in the Gemini app (paid plans), Google Flow, or the Gemini API, keep your artist visually consistent with up to 3 reference images per generation, and extend key shots in 7-second steps up to a hard ceiling of 148 seconds. The critical limitation: Veo 3.1 cannot accept your music track as an input — it generates its own audio, which you mute — so the sync to your song happens in a video editor, not in the model. Budget roughly $0.05–$0.40 per generated second on the API, or use a Google AI plan's Flow credits.

What can Veo 3.1 actually do for a music video?

Veo 3.1 is Google DeepMind's video model, and its raw image quality is the reason musicians keep asking about it. It generates video with native audio, supports up to 4K output, and — most useful for music work — accepts reference images so your artist looks like the same person from shot to shot, per Google DeepMind's Veo page.

Where can you access Veo 3.1 — and what does it cost?

Veo 3.1 is available in the Gemini app, Google Flow, Google AI Studio, and the Gemini API, per DeepMind's availability list. There are three practical routes for a musician:

  • Google AI plans (Gemini app + Flow). As of July 2026, Google's plan page lists video generation on its paid tiers: Google AI Plus at $4.99/month with 200 Flow credits, Google AI Pro at $19.99/month with 1,000 Flow credits, and Google AI Ultra from $99.99/month with 10,000–25,000 Flow credits and Veo 3.1 access called out by name. Flow is Google's filmmaking interface — the friendliest place to storyboard, generate, and extend shots without touching code.
  • Gemini API (pay per second). Per Google's API pricing page (July 2026): Veo 3.1 Standard costs $0.40/second at 720p/1080p and $0.60/second at 4K; Veo 3.1 Fast costs $0.10/second at 720p; Veo 3.1 Lite costs $0.05/second at 720p. Audio is included. Note that video extension only works on Standard and Fast — not Lite.
  • Google AI Studio. A middle path: the API models in a browser interface, useful for testing prompts before committing to a batch.

What's the catch for musicians?

Here it is, plainly: Veo 3.1 has no music-track input. The API documentation lists text, images, and video (for extensions) as inputs — there is no way to upload your song and have the model generate motion that follows it. Veo generates its own audio (dialogue, sound effects, ambience) on every clip, and on Veo 3.1 models that native audio is always on. For a music video, you mute all of it.

This is the structural difference from Seedance 2.0, which accepts a music track as a reference input and generates against it — the main reason our Seedance workflow feels built for musicians while the Veo workflow feels adapted for them. With Veo, rhythm and sync are entirely your job in the edit, which is why the beat-sync editing guide is not optional reading for this tutorial — it's step five.

The honest framing: you're choosing between Veo's image quality and reference-image control versus Seedance's music-aware generation and 15-second clips. Neither is wrong. But know which trade you're making before you spend money.

How do you make a music video with Veo 3.1, step by step?

Step 1: Storyboard from your song's structure

Print your song's sections — intro, verses, choruses, bridge — with timestamps. Since Veo generates at most 8 seconds per clip (148 seconds with extensions, 720p only), a 3-minute song means roughly 20–35 distinct shots at music-video cutting pace. Assign each section a visual idea and each shot a single action. One subject doing one thing per shot generates far more reliably than crowded scenes. Decide now which 2–3 shots deserve to be long extended takes and which are quick cuts.

Step 2: Build your reference images

Generate or photograph up to 3 reference images per shot: your artist (or animated character), a style frame, and a key object or location. Reference images are how the same face survives 30 separate generations — the difference between a music video and a slideshow of strangers. Note the constraint from Google's docs: generations using reference images must be 8 seconds. Our character consistency guide covers how to shoot and select reference images that actually hold.

Step 3: Write camera-first prompts

Google's official Veo 3.1 prompting guide structures prompts around subject, action, style, and — crucially — camera positioning and motion. For music videos, lead with the camera: "slow dolly-in on a singer under a single sodium streetlight, shallow depth of field, 35mm, rain" reads as a shot; "a sad singer in the rain" reads as a mood board. Specify one camera move per clip, matched to the energy of the song section it will land on — slow push-ins for verses, whips and crash zooms for choruses. Our camera movement prompt library maps specific moves to musical moments.

Step 4: Generate at 8 seconds, extend only where it earns it

Generate everything at 8 seconds first — it's the required duration for reference images, extensions, and higher resolutions anyway. Expect to regenerate: two to three takes per shot is normal before one is usable. Then extend only your designated long takes, 7 seconds at a time. Remember the trade: extended chains are 720p only, so if you want a 4K hero shot, it stays a standalone 8-second clip. If your concept genuinely needs long unbroken takes, read our guide to longer AI videos before committing to Veo — the 148-second ceiling is real, and Kling 3.0 reaches roughly 3 minutes if that's the deciding factor (see our Kling vs Seedance comparison).

Step 5: Assemble the beat-cut edit

Import everything into your editor (CapCut, DaVinci Resolve, Premiere — any of them work), drop your actual song on the timeline, mute every Veo clip's native audio, and cut to the beat. This is where the video becomes a music video: place your hardest visual hits on downbeats, hold longer shots through verses, and accelerate cut rate into the final chorus. The full technique — markers, cut-rate maps, J-cuts around vocal entries — is in the beat-sync editing guide.

Is Veo 3.1 the right tool for your music video?

Choose Veo 3.1 if your concept is cinematic and shot-driven: strong reference-image control, up-to-4K standalone shots, and prompting that rewards precise camera language. Choose something else if your concept depends on the model responding to the music itself — Veo can't hear your track, and no amount of prompting changes that. Performance-sync and rhythm-native concepts are better served by tools with audio input, and long single-take concepts by tools with higher ceilings. Frequently asked questions

Can Veo 3.1 sync a video to my song automatically?+

No. As of July 2026, Veo 3.1's inputs are text, images, and video (for extensions) only — there is no way to upload a music track. Veo generates its own audio, which you mute. Sync happens in your video editor: you cut the generated clips to your song's beat manually. Tools like Seedance 2.0 differ here by accepting a music track as a reference input.

How long can a Veo 3.1 video be?+

A single generation is 4, 6, or 8 seconds. You can extend a clip 7 seconds at a time, up to 20 extensions, for a maximum of 148 seconds total — and extended videos are limited to 720p resolution. A full-length music video is therefore assembled from many separate clips in an editor rather than generated as one piece.

Do I need Google AI Ultra to use Veo 3.1?+

No. As of July 2026, Google's plan page lists video generation on paid tiers starting with Google AI Plus ($4.99/month, 200 Flow credits) and Pro ($19.99/month, 1,000 Flow credits); Ultra (from $99.99/month) names Veo 3.1 explicitly with 10,000–25,000 Flow credits. Alternatively, the Gemini API bills per second with no subscription: $0.05–$0.40 per second depending on tier and resolution.

How much does a Veo 3.1 music video cost via the API?+

At Google's July 2026 API rates, an 8-second shot costs $0.40 on Veo 3.1 Lite (720p), $0.80 on Fast (720p), or $3.20 on Standard (720p/1080p). A 3-minute video built from about 30 shots with two to three takes each lands roughly $24–$36 on Lite, $48–$72 on Fast, or $192–$288 on Standard.

Does Veo 3.1 generate its own audio, and can I turn it off?+

Veo 3.1 models always generate native audio — dialogue, sound effects, and ambience — with every clip; there is no off switch at generation time. For music videos this doesn't matter: you mute the clips' audio in your editor and lay your own track on the timeline. The native audio is only useful if you want a spoken intro or diegetic sound effect moment inside the video.

Is Veo 3.1 better than Seedance 2.0 for music videos?+

They trade different strengths. Veo 3.1 offers up-to-4K standalone shots, up to 3 reference images for character consistency, and strong camera-language prompting — but no music input and a 148-second extension ceiling at 720p. Seedance 2.0 accepts your music track as a reference input, generates clips up to 15 seconds, and chains extensions, with plans from $9.90–$59.90/month as of July 2026. Shot-driven cinematic concepts favor Veo; music-reactive concepts favor Seedance.
