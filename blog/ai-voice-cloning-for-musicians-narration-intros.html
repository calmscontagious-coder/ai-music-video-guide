Instant or professional cloning: which one does a musician need?

Instant cloning is the fast route: the product page says it works with 1 to 5 minutes of audio, and the docs recommend 1 to 2 minutes. Professional cloning needs 30 or more minutes of clean audio and is only available on the Creator plan or above (ElevenLabs voice cloning page and docs, read November 2, 2026).

What instant cloning needs

The product page gives two numbers that look contradictory at first. It says you can clone a voice with 1 to 5 minutes of audio, and elsewhere that a voice can be created "using just a 10-second recording" (ElevenLabs voice cloning page, read November 2, 2026).

The docs settle it: aim for 1 to 2 minutes of good audio, because "Having more than 2-3 minutes of audio will yield little improvement". Each file must be at least 5 seconds long and all samples together at least 10 seconds (ElevenLabs voice cloning guide, read November 2, 2026).

Format matters less than you might expect. The guide recommends MP3 at 192 kbps or higher and says uncompressed formats such as WAV "usually do not improve clone quality" (ElevenLabs voice cloning guide, read November 2, 2026).

What professional cloning needs

Professional cloning asks for far more audio. The product page says it "Requires 30+ minutes of clean audio samples", its FAQ recommends at least 30 minutes with about 3 hours being best, and the docs put the range at 30 to 180 minutes of good audio (ElevenLabs voice cloning page and guide, read November 2, 2026). Once your samples are uploaded and your voice is verified, fine-tuning takes an estimated 3 to 6 hours, so this is not a same-session job.

Record the way you plan to narrate, because verification asks you to speak in a style similar to the training audio.

The product page lists voice verification and enterprise-grade security as part of professional cloning. On languages it is inconsistent: it says clones "can automatically speak 32+ languages" in one place and "90+ languages" in another, while the docs say professional clones support the Eleven v4 model family, 90+ languages in total (ElevenLabs voice cloning page and guide, read November 2, 2026). Treat the model-version claim as current to the page date.

What neither page says about singing

Neither cloning page mentions singing or music. The product page and the guide cover audio samples, formats, slots, verification and languages. If your plan is to clone your voice so it can sing demos, the pages we read do not promise that, and we will not either.

How do credits work for narration, dubs and voice changes?

Narration draws on the credit pool, which the pricing page describes only as an approximate number of text to speech minutes: Starter's 30k credits is about 30 minutes, Creator's 121k about 121 minutes (ElevenLabs pricing page, read November 2, 2026). Those are approximations, not a fixed rate. Dubbing and voice changes are priced per minute of audio, listed below.

The per-feature rates on the pricing page (read November 2, 2026):

  • Automatic dubbing: 2,000 credits per minute with a watermark, 3,000 credits per minute without.
  • Dubbing Studio: 5,000 credits per minute with a watermark, 10,000 credits per minute without.
  • Voice Changer: 1,000 credits per minute.
  • Voice Isolator: 1,000 credits per minute.
  • Eleven Music: 900 credits per minute.

The page gives no dollar-per-minute figure and we will not compute one, but the shape is clear. A channel intro read by your clone draws from the text to speech pool. A dubbed behind-the-scenes video is different: Dubbing Studio without a watermark costs 10,000 credits per minute against Starter's 30k credits a month (ElevenLabs pricing page, read November 2, 2026), so Starter's monthly credits cover only a few minutes of watermark-free Dubbing Studio output.

Can you clone a bandmate's voice, and what does verification involve?

Not as a professional clone. The docs say "You can only create a Professional Voice Clone of your own voice" and "Even with their consent, you cannot clone someone else's voice". The product page's FAQ says cloning someone else's voice "requires their explicit consent" (ElevenLabs voice cloning page and guide, read November 2, 2026).

The consent line is the general rule; the own-voice-only line is specific to professional clones. For a band that wants every member's voice on the channel, each member runs their own professional clone on their own account. If the voice belongs to a persona rather than a person, our virtual artist AI branding guide covers the identity side.

How verification works

Verification is the step that proves the professional clone is you. The guide's process: allow microphone access, speak in a style similar to the training audio, read each verification line once, then press Stop. After failed attempts you wait 24 hours or contact support, and an unverified professional clone can be deleted 24 hours after the most recent verification attempt (ElevenLabs voice cloning guide, read November 2, 2026). Train and verify in the same speaking style.

Plan the timing: a failed verification adds a 24-hour wait, and fine-tuning after verification takes an estimated 3 to 6 hours, so start days before a release.

Do you have to label a YouTube video narrated by your cloned voice?

No, for your own voice. YouTube says creators don't need to disclose "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs". You do need to disclose realistic AI content that makes a real person appear to say something they didn't, or that "Creates music that's the main focus of the video" (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026).

The disclosure page (which shows only "2026 Google" as a date) lists four triggers for realistic altered or synthetic content (read November 2, 2026):

  • "Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't"
  • "Alters footage of a real event or place"
  • "Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur"
  • "Creates music that's the main focus of the video"

No disclosure is needed for non-realistic AI content, for "primarily aesthetic" edits such as beauty filters, or for "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs" (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026). A channel intro, a studio voiceover or a dub in your own cloned voice is on the no-label list.

When a label is needed, you select "Yes" under "AI use" in YouTube Studio; for AI music or photorealistic content it may appear in the video player, for non-music AI audio or animated content in the expanded description. YouTube may apply labels automatically, and the page says disclosure alone doesn't affect audience reach or monetization eligibility (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026).

The catch for musicians is the fourth trigger: narration needs no label, but a video whose main focus is AI-generated music does.
