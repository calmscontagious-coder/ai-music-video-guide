Who Owns an AI Music Video? Copyright, Tool Terms, What You Keep (2026)

By the AI Music Video Guide team · Updated October 27, 2026 · 7 min read

This post contains affiliate links — if you sign up through them we may earn a commission at no extra cost to you (disclosure). Researched and edited for accuracy with AI assistance.

Quick answer: In the US, nobody owns the copyright in a song or video that AI generated from a prompt alone; the sources we read say it is public domain. You own what a human made: lyrics, a composed melody, a sung vocal, a creative edit. Tool terms grant permission to use the output, not copyright. This is not legal advice.

AI music video copyright matters the week you release. If you cannot own the clip, you cannot stop a stranger re-uploading it or defend it when a claim lands. We read four sources: a UK composer's explainer updated July 31, 2026, a Dutch law firm's EU overview, an AI music vendor's account of the 2025 appeals ruling, and a musician's January 2026 video. We are not lawyers; for a real dispute, pay for an hour with one.

Who owns a fully AI-generated music video in the US?

Nobody. The US Copyright Office's January 2025 guidance, as Silverman Sound summarizes it (updated July 31, 2026), holds that 100 percent AI-generated content cannot be copyrighted and falls into the public domain, and writing the prompt does not make you the author. The rule is about content, not format, so a prompt-only video clip sits where a prompt-only song sits: unprotected.

The Office's words, as quoted by Silverman Sound: "The outputs of generative AI can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements." Soundverse, an AI music vendor writing in 2025, agrees: fully AI-generated compositions are public domain, "freely available for anyone to use or reproduce."

Silverman Sound spells out the cost. With no copyright, nothing stops someone registering your track with a fingerprinting service such as Content ID, after which every video using it can be claimed and the revenue redirected. The author implies it happened to him.

What did the court ruling the sources discuss decide?

It confirmed that copyright requires a human author. Silverman Sound names the case as Thaler v Perlmutter, affirmed on appeal in 2025, and says it settled that a prompt alone is not authorship. Soundverse describes the same US Court of Appeals decision from March 2025 as drawing "a clear legal boundary": works created entirely by AI cannot be owned under copyright law.

What it left open is how much human input is enough. Soundverse says adjusting AI-generated melodies, writing lyrics, or structuring an arrangement could meet the threshold, and calls the amount needed "a legal grey area." Silverman Sound passes on the Copyright Office's registration advice: disclose the AI-generated portions, and expect protection to attach to human selection, arrangement, and creative modifications.

How do the EU and the Netherlands treat AI music and video?

The same way on authorship, with a stricter line on training. AIRecht, a Dutch law firm (2025, read October 25, 2026), writes that EU case law protects a work only when it is a human's original creative result, so an entirely AI-generated work is "generally considered to be in the public domain." Hybrid works qualify when the human made free and creative choices that show in the result.

Three EU specifics from AIRecht. The 2019 DSM Directive lets rights holders opt out of commercial text-and-data mining, and Buma/Stemra, the Dutch collecting society, has opted out its whole repertoire while adding registration procedures for partially AI-generated works. The EU AI Act, adopted July 2024 with obligations from August 2025, requires generative AI providers to summarize training data, label AI output, and keep a copyright policy.

Silverman Sound adds two dated European facts. On July 31, 2026, a Munich court ruled that Suno infringed copyright in a case brought by the German collecting society GEMA; Suno is weighing an appeal. Article 50 of the EU AI Act becomes enforceable on August 2, 2026, requiring machine-readable marking of AI-generated audio.

What do tool terms of service actually give you?

Permission, not ownership. Silverman Sound quotes Suno's terms: "Due to the nature of machine learning, Suno makes no representation or warranty to you that any copyright will vest in any Output." Commercial-use rights mean the vendor will not stop you monetizing; they do not let you stop anyone else, and no contract can grant a copyright the law withholds.

Brent Brown Music, in a video posted January 8, 2026, puts it plainly: "if you just generate a song and somebody else takes it and re-uploads it as their own, there's really not much you can do about that." The video carries its own not-legal-advice disclaimer.

Our sources do not quote the terms of any video generator, so we will not characterize them. They do say that AI platform terms shift liability to the user (Silverman Sound), and that contracts can assign AI output to the human creator "as far as possible" or to the party that deployed the AI (AIRecht). Which layers of an AI music video can you own?

Split the video into layers and the picture clears. Human-written lyrics, a human-sung vocal, a composed melody and a creative edit are protectable; prompt-generated audio and footage are not, per the US guidance our sources describe. What should you do before you publish an AI music video?

Write your own lyrics, keep every draft, make real changes, disclose AI use, and read the terms of each tool you used. These steps come from the sources, and none of them creates copyright in the generated parts. They give you something you can own and a paper trail to prove it.

  1. Write the lyrics yourself. Silverman Sound: lyrics can be copyrighted even if the music cannot, provided a human wrote them. Brent Brown would register them separately.
  2. Document everything. Soundverse recommends screenshots, draft versions and production notes. Silverman Sound adds prompts and every modification: "this documentation might be your only defence."
  3. Make substantial human changes. Soundverse lists restructuring a beat, adding live instrumentation, reworking lyrics. Brent Brown's three levels: chop and rearrange the stems; add real percussion or vocals on top as a watermark; or start from a song you can play and sing, register it, then use AI as the backing band.
  4. Avoid artist-name prompts and cloned voices. Silverman Sound calls "in the style of" prompts a legal minefield. Our page on AI cover songs, voice clones and what gets removed covers the platform side.
  5. Disclose AI at upload. Per Silverman Sound, Spotify adopted the DDEX AI-disclosure standard in September 2025, Apple Music made Transparency Tags a delivery requirement from March 2026, covering artwork, audio, composition and music video, and Deezer has tagged more than 13 million AI tracks. Our list of which platforms accept AI music has the rules per service.

Disclosure is not optional. Silverman Sound reports Deezer's finding that 85 percent of streams on AI tracks were detected as fraudulent, Spotify's removal of around 75 million spam tracks in twelve months, and a Deezer-Ipsos survey in which 80 percent of listeners wanted AI music labelled while 97 percent could not tell the difference unaided.

Your next step: before the next upload, open a text file, paste your lyrics with today's date, list every edit you made to the generated audio and footage, and save the prompts beside it. Then save a dated copy of the terms of the song tool and the video tool you used. For the October 2026 licensing picture, read the musicians union's AI demands and what you can still release.

Frequently asked questions

Can I copyright a music video made entirely with AI?+

Not in the US, according to the sources we read. The Copyright Office's January 2025 guidance, as Silverman Sound summarizes it, says 100 percent AI-generated content cannot be copyrighted and is public domain, and a prompt does not make you the author. The protectable parts are the ones a human made: lyrics, a performance, a composed melody, or a creative edit of the generated footage.

If I wrote the lyrics, do I own the song an AI tool made from them?+

You own the lyrics. Silverman Sound states that human-written lyrics can be copyrighted even when the music underneath cannot, and Brent Brown suggests registering them as a separate claim. The generated audio stays unprotected unless you add melody, performance, or substantial arrangement of your own, and the sources give no fixed test for how much is enough.

Does a paid commercial plan give me the copyright?+

No. A commercial plan is the vendor's permission to monetize, and Silverman Sound quotes Suno's own terms disclaiming any warranty that copyright will vest in an output. Commercial rights stop the tool company objecting to your release; they do not let you stop a third party from reusing the same file. Read each tool's terms, and keep a dated copy.

Is the rule different in Europe?+

Not on authorship. AIRecht writes that EU law also requires a human's original creative result, so a fully AI-generated work is generally public domain there too. Europe is stricter on training: rights holders can opt out of text-and-data mining, Buma/Stemra has opted out its whole repertoire, and the EU AI Act requires providers to label AI output and summarize training data.

Can someone legally re-upload my AI-generated video or song?+

If the work is entirely AI-generated, the sources say yes, because there is no copyright to enforce. Silverman Sound describes the worst case: a stranger registers the track with a fingerprinting service and claims every video that uses it. Your defence is the human layer you can prove, so keep lyric drafts, edit lists, prompts, and session files with dates.

Do I have to label an AI music video as AI-made?+

Increasingly, yes. Per Silverman Sound, Apple Music's Transparency Tags became a delivery requirement in March 2026 and cover artwork, audio, composition and music video; Spotify adopted the DDEX disclosure standard in September 2025; and Article 50 of the EU AI Act, enforceable from August 2, 2026, requires machine-readable marking of AI-generated audio. Undisclosed AI is what gets releases pulled.
