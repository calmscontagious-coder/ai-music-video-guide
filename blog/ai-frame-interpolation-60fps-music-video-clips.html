Frame Interpolation and 60fps Smoothing for AI Music Video Clips

What does frame interpolation do to an AI music video clip?

It generates new frames between the ones you already have, so motion can look smoother at a higher frame rate or play back slower without obvious stutter. One tutorial frames it as animation: existing frames would equate to key poses, with interpolated frames added between them (YouTube tutorial on frame interpolation, read November 3, 2026).

Smoothing or slow motion?

One tutorial we read walks through Topaz Video AI 4 turning 60 FPS footage into slow-motion sequences (YouTube tutorial on Topaz Video AI 4 frame interpolation, read November 3, 2026). That's a camera workflow, but the planning habit carries over: decide what you want from interpolation before you render, not after.

For a music video the practical choice is between two uses of the same tool. You can raise the frame rate of a clip so motion looks smoother at normal speed, or you can stretch the clip into slow motion. Both depend on invented in-between frames, so both inherit whatever mistakes the tool makes. The tutorial is about camera footage, so applying it to generated clips is our extrapolation, not something the video shows.

Duplicate frames and over-aggressive settings

That tutorial warns that pushing the setting higher may remove and replace similar looking frames, not just the duplicated ones (YouTube tutorial on frame interpolation, read November 3, 2026). For AI clips with deliberate holds, such as a freeze on a snare hit, we'd compare the interpolated shot against the original before committing the render to the whole edit.

How does Topaz handle 24fps to 60fps smoothing?

Morphic's catalog page for Topaz Video says frame interpolation can raise 24fps footage to 60fps or higher, and that the same tool upscales up to 4x (Morphic, read November 13, 2026). That page is a third-party description of the model, not a test, so we treat it as a capability claim.

What the catalog page lists

Morphic's entry describes the model as it appears in its own catalog, so it isn't a version-by-version spec sheet. Besides interpolation and upscaling, it lists video stabilization and temporal consistency, described as keeping visual consistency from frame to frame. Those read like vendor-style descriptions, and we haven't verified them on generated footage.

There's also a naming wrinkle. The tutorials we read show Topaz Video AI, and one of them covers version 4 (YouTube tutorial on Topaz Video AI 4 frame interpolation, read November 3, 2026). The catalog and the vendor pages say Topaz Video. The notes don't say how those names map to each other or which build ships which feature, so check your installed version before following a tutorial's menus.

What the vendor said about its models

In a March 2023 roadmap update, the vendor wrote that a new version of its Apollo model produces significantly more natural results for difficult footage, and that it outperforms the older Apollo and Chronos models and decisively surpasses optical flow for footage with nonlinear motion (Topaz Community roadmap post, March 2023).

The same post says the team was quite satisfied with the quality of its frame interpolation and would refocus on its general enhancement models (Topaz Community roadmap post, March 2023). That's a vendor view from years before our read date, so it tells us where the model stood then, not what ships today. Nonlinear motion is the phrase that matters to us, though. Spins, whip pans and uneven dance movement are common in music video footage, so that's where we'd test first.

From the notes, Topaz documents interpolation across several pages and tutorials, while HitPaw VikPea appears through one press release about its V5.1.0 frame interpolation upgrade, aimed at sports, gaming and fast transitions (HitPaw announcement on Yahoo Finance, read November 13, 2026). Reading the HitPaw announcement

The release says the upgraded model creates smoother, more natural motion in high-action videos and aims to reduce motion blur, stuttering and ghosting (HitPaw announcement on Yahoo Finance, read November 13, 2026). It's a launch announcement, so it lists benefits, not measurements. Ghosting is the word to remember. It's the visible double image that can appear when a tool guesses wrong between two frames, and it's a flaw to look for in any tool you try.

How to read the comparison

The table is thin because the evidence is thin. Topaz has more documentation in our notes, but more documentation isn't the same as better results. HitPaw's release focuses on high-action content, which resembles many performance and dance shots, while the Topaz material leans on camera footage and slow motion. Neither source tests AI-generated clips, so the useful comparison is the one you run yourself on a single hard shot.

How should we fit interpolation into an AI music video edit?

Pick final takes first, fix visible artifacts, trim each shot to the length you'll use, then interpolate and assemble the beat-synced cut. That order means render time goes only to shots that survive the edit. It's our workflow judgment, not a vendor recommendation.

A working order

  • Choose final takes. Our guide to editing AI clips into a beat-synced music video covers selection and cut points.
  • Fix the source first. Hands, faces and flicker are source problems. See fixing AI video artifacts in hands and faces, because a smoother warped hand is still a warped hand.
  • Trim. Cut each shot to its used length so you don't spend cloud credits on frames you'll discard.
  • Interpolate and compare. Render the shot, then play it beside the original. Keep the original if the difference looks worse.
  • Assemble. Keep one project frame rate so smoothed and unsmoothed shots don't fight each other on the timeline.

If a clip is too short before you smooth it, extend it first. Slowing a clip down is not new content, and our AI video extender comparison covers the alternatives. For timing the cuts, beat sync for AI video editing is the companion read.

Shots to check by eye

Fast motion is the obvious test, since HitPaw's pitch and the Topaz roadmap claim both center on difficult or high-action movement. Static holds are the second, given the tutorial's warning about similar looking frames being replaced. Anything with hands, faces or text is the third, because interpolation could smear detail that was already fragile.

Gaps we'd fill with a test

  • Whether interpolated AI clips show ghosting or warped detail that the source didn't have.
  • How many credits one cloud render spends, since the notes give tiers but no per-render rate.

Pick the hardest shot in your video, run it through whichever tool you're considering, and judge the result at full screen with the song playing. A smoothing pass that looks fine on a muted thumbnail can fall apart against the beat.

Frequently asked questions

Does frame interpolation fix flicker in AI video clips?

We found nothing in our sources showing that it does. Interpolation adds in-between frames, and one catalog page lists temporal consistency as a separate feature, which we haven't verified on generated clips. Treat flicker as a source problem, fix or regenerate the shot first, and test one smoothed clip before rendering the rest.

Can I raise a clip to 60fps without it looking strange?

Sometimes, but results depend on the motion. A vendor tutorial warns that higher settings may remove and replace similar looking frames, not only duplicated ones, so deliberate holds can change. Compare each smoothed shot against the original, and test fast motion and still moments before committing a whole video.

Do cloud credits roll over for frame interpolation renders?

On the vendor pricing page we read, monthly cloud video credits expire at month end and do not roll over. That applies to cloud rendering there. The sources don't say how many credits a single render uses, so we can't estimate a cost per clip and won't guess one.

Can I use smoothed clips in a commercial music video?

The vendor license we read covers personal and commercial use for organizations under a stated annual revenue threshold, and export limits apply to its web products only. Check the threshold against whoever owns the release, and confirm the terms on the current page before you publish, because we only saw them on one day.

Is there a head-to-head test of smoothing tools on AI clips?

Not in the sources we read. We found vendor pages, a press release, a catalog entry and tutorials, but no side-by-side test on AI-generated video, so any ranking would be opinion. Run the same short, fast shot through each tool and compare the results against the original.
