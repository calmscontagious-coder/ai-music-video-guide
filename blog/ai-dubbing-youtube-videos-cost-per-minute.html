AI dubbing for YouTube videos: cost per minute and how to add the track

Quick answer: AI dubbing for YouTube videos costs $2.70, $2.45 or $2.23 per extra minute on ElevenLabs' Starter, Creator and Pro plans, which include 2, 9 and 44 minutes a month; Free includes 0.4 minutes at $4.91 (ElevenLabs dubbing page, read November 2, 2026). The dub keeps your own voice, and YouTube takes it as an extra audio track.

If you talk on camera, a dubbed version of each video is a cheap way to reach a second audience without re-recording anything. This guide is for the independent artist with vlogs, interviews, lyric explainers and tutorials: what the automatic dub does to your voice, what a minute costs on each plan, how credits behave when you skip a month, and the exact clicks to attach the track in YouTube Studio.

Everything here is about speech. Nothing in our notes covers dubbing sung vocals, so treat a dubbed chorus as untested until you try one.

Does an AI dub keep my own voice in the new language?

Yes, on the service we cover. ElevenLabs says each translation is delivered in a voice clone of the original speaker, that the cloning is automatic, and that it preserves the speaker's identity, pitch and tone (ElevenLabs dubbing page, read November 2, 2026). The page lists support for 90+ languages and accents under Dubbing v2.

Your audience knows your voice, and a generic narrator in Spanish or Japanese sounds like a different channel. A clone of your own voice reading the translation keeps the identity intact, which is also why YouTube treats it differently at disclosure time.

ElevenLabs sells dubbing two ways, per its dubbing page (read November 2, 2026). ElevenCreative is described as "One-click dubbing for creators, marketers, and teams" with no setup. ElevenProductions is "For studios and broadcasters with broadcast-quality requirements" and adds human translators, expert voice casting and professional mixing. For a solo channel the first one is the relevant product. The page gives no price for the second.

Two gaps the published pages don't close

First, the dubbing page does not state a maximum file length, so we can't tell you whether a long interview goes through in one pass. Second, the voice clone described there is built for spoken content.

We found nothing on the page about dubbing sung vocals, pitch-matched melodies or harmonies, so if the plan is to translate a chorus, run one short clip before you budget for an album. Our guide on how to make TikTok videos with AI covers the short-form side.

What happens to unused credits if I only dub some months?

On paid plans, up to twice your usual credit quota rolls over automatically into the next month as long as you stay on the same plan. The help center's example is a Starter account with 60,000 unused credits that renews with a 90,000 credit quota (ElevenLabs help center, read November 2, 2026).

That rollover is the detail that makes an artist's irregular schedule workable. Unused credits roll over up to the cap, so a quiet month does not waste the whole allowance. In the help center's example the 90,000 figure is 60,000 rolled over plus 30,000 new (ElevenLabs help center, read November 2, 2026).

The same help page sets out the edges of the rule, and they are where people lose credits:

  • Cancelling or downgrading takes effect at the end of the current billing cycle, and the page says you will lose any unused credits when this happens.
  • Upgrading mid-cycle keeps what you have: any remaining unused credits are added to the new quota, and the billing cycle resets.
  • Pay As You Go credits expire 12 months after purchase and are not affected by the monthly rollover.

Because the cap is twice the usual quota, credits stop building past that point, so a very long gap between releases still wastes some. Keep the plan that matches your average month and let the rollover absorb the busy ones; upgrading for one album rollout and downgrading afterwards forfeits whatever is left. The full wording is on the ElevenLabs help center page about end-of-month quota.

How do I attach the dubbed track on YouTube?

Through YouTube Studio's Languages page, if your channel has Advanced features. YouTube says multi-language audio is currently available to creators with access to Advanced features, that the file must be audio-only and roughly the same length as the video, and that an existing auto dub in that language must be deleted first (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026).

The desktop steps, as YouTube's help page lists them:

  • Log in to YouTube Studio.
  • Open Languages in the left menu.
  • Select the video.
  • Click "Add Language" and choose the language.
  • Next to "Dub", click "Add".
  • Click "Select file".
  • Choose the audio file.
  • Click "Publish".

The file rule is worth quoting exactly: "Files must be in a supported audio-only file format and roughly the same length as your video." So you upload the dubbed audio on its own, not a second video. Export an audio-only file from your dubbing tool and keep it the same length as the original. The page lists no supported formats, so if an upload is refused, the format is the first thing to check.

Two more points from the same page. The feature "does not automatically create these tracks, unlike the automatic dubbing feature", so the dub you made elsewhere is the one viewers hear. And if YouTube has already generated an auto dub in that language, "you first have to delete the auto dub in that language before you can upload your own dub". The full steps are on YouTube Help's Multi-language features page, which also gives no list of auto-dubbing languages.

The Advanced features gate is the part we can't fix for you. YouTube's page says multi-language audio is currently available to creators with access to Advanced features, so check that your channel has it before you spend on dubbing. Do I have to label an AI dub of my own voice?

No, for your own voice. YouTube's disclosure page lists "Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs" among the uses that need no disclosure (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026). A clone of someone else's voice is different: making a real person appear to say something they didn't is on the must-disclose list.

The page's must-disclose list covers realistic AI-generated or altered content that does any of the following, quoted from the page:

  • "Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do"
  • "Alters footage of a real event or place"
  • "Generates a realistic scene that didn't actually occur"
  • "Creates music that's the main focus of the video"

A dub where only your own voice is cloned sits outside all four. An interview is different. The dubbing page says each translation is delivered in a voice clone of the original speaker, so a guest's voice gets cloned too, and YouTube's exemption names only cloning one's own voice. A guest's cloned voice in an interview dub is not covered by that exemption, so check the four must-disclose items for that case.

The page is explicit that "Creators don't need to disclose non-realistic content that's made with AI" and that minor edits that are "primarily aesthetic", such as beauty filters and color adjustments, need nothing either. Where it bites an artist is the last item: if the video exists to carry an AI-generated song, that is a disclosure case regardless of how the voice-over was made. Our guide to AI music video rules on TikTok, Spotify and Instagram covers the other platforms' versions of the same rule.

When you do need to disclose, the mechanism is a single setting: select "Yes" under "AI use" in YouTube Studio, which adds a label. For AI music or photorealistic content the label may appear in the video player; for non-music AI audio or animated content it may appear in the expanded description. YouTube may also apply labels automatically. The page says disclosure alone doesn't affect audience reach or monetization eligibility. The wording is on YouTube Help's page on disclosing use of GenAI content.

What about the video itself and other platforms?

The dub only covers audio, so you still need the picture. Camera footage covers a tutorial; an AI video model can supply the rest. Spotify for Creators' video specs, written for podcast video, describe one video track and one audio track of the same duration (read November 2, 2026), so plan on a separate upload per language there.

If the same video goes to Spotify, its video specs (read November 2, 2026) ask for an MP4 container (MOV is also compatible; the page says "We discourage FLV and AVI"), H.264 or H.265 (HEVC), 1080p or higher, and AAC-LC, PCM or FLAC audio at 128 Kbps or higher in stereo. Recommended duration is less than 4 hours, with up to 12 hours compatible. Because that spec allows one audio track, the dubbed edition is its own file there.

In YouTube Studio, open Languages in the left menu, select the video, click Add Language and pick the language, then click Add next to Dub, click Select file, choose the audio file and click Publish. The file must be audio-only and roughly the same length as the video, and the feature is available to channels with Advanced features (YouTube Help, read November 2, 2026).
