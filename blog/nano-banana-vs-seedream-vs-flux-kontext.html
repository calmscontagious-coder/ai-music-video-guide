Nano Banana vs SeeDream 4 vs Flux Kontext: Which AI Image Engine for Artist Visuals?

Why does the engine matter more than the prompt?

Because the same brief fails differently on different engines: one drifts your facial features, one nails the face but flattens the lighting, one holds everything except readable type. The prompt is constant; the model decides identity preservation, text rendering, and cross-image consistency — the three capabilities artist visuals stress hardest. Pick the engine for the job first, then write the prompt.

For artist visuals, three jobs recur constantly: editing existing press photos without losing your identity, generating cover art (often with typography), and building the still keyframes you hand to a video generator. Each job stresses a different capability — identity preservation, text rendering, and cross-image consistency — and no single engine leads at all three. This piece is the decision layer above prompting: which engine to select before you type a word.

The contenders in 60 seconds each

Nano Banana (Google). Google's image family from the Gemini line. The base model is built for fast conversational editing; the Pro version raises the ceiling considerably. According to Google's Nano Banana Pro announcement, the Pro model is built on Gemini 3 Pro, outputs at 2K and 4K, renders legible in-image text in multiple languages, blends up to 14 input images, and maintains the resemblance of up to 5 people across edits. Inside Seedance, base Nano Banana costs 1 credit per render — the cheapest engine on the platform — with a Pro mode toggle for 1K/2K/4K output and renders that typically land in under two seconds.

SeeDream 4 (ByteDance). The engine slot is labeled SeeDream 4 in Seedance's tool switcher and currently runs Seedream 4.5, ByteDance's December 2025 update. According to Runware's Seedream 4.5 documentation, the model generates and edits at 2K to 4K and supports up to 14 reference images. According to getimg.ai's Seedream 4.5 guide, 4.5 specifically improved small-text rendering and distant faces over 4.0. On Seedance it handles both text-to-image and instruction-based editing at a flat 3 credits per render, whether you pick 2K or 4K quality.

Flux Kontext (Black Forest Labs). A different architecture philosophy: Kontext unifies generation and in-context editing in one flow model. According to Black Forest Labs, its core strengths are character consistency across multiple scenes and edits without any fine-tuning, local edits that leave the rest of the frame untouched, and competitive typography — at inference speeds up to 8x faster than comparable models.

The sibling: Flux Krea. Also selectable in Seedance's image tools, Krea is Kontext's generation-focused relative, co-developed by BFL and Krea AI. It's deliberately "opinionated": per the FLUX.1 Krea release notes, it was trained to avoid the oversaturated, waxy "AI look." If you're generating moody band imagery from scratch — no editing, no text — Krea produces the least AI-flavored frames of the four.

Four artist jobs, one identical brief each

Test 1: Press-shot edit with identity preservation

The brief: take your actual press photo, swap the background to a lit stage, keep your face untouched. This is the job where engines diverge most. Nano Banana's edit pipeline was designed for exactly this — targeted changes with face lock — and at 1 credit per attempt you can afford ten rolls to get one keeper. Flux Kontext is the closest competitor: its no-fine-tuning character consistency holds a face through successive edits, which matters when you change background first, then lighting, then wardrobe in separate passes. SeeDream 4.5's instruction editing is respectable and its distant-face handling improved in 4.5, but at 3 credits per roll it's the more expensive iteration loop for a job that usually needs several attempts.

Test 2: Cover-art generation with typography

The brief: a photorealistic cover with the album title rendered on the artwork. Text is where most image models still embarrass themselves. Nano Banana Pro's multi-language text rendering is the headline capability here — Google explicitly positions it for posters and layouts with legible type. Seedream 4.5's small-text improvements make it a genuine second option, and its photoreal bias means the imagery around the type looks like photography rather than illustration. Kontext's typography is workable but it isn't the reason you'd pick it. Practical tip: generate at 4K (Nano Banana Pro mode or SeeDream's High setting) so the master comfortably downsamples to distributor cover-art specs. Pair whichever engine you choose with the prompt patterns in our album cover art prompt guide.

Test 3: Style-consistent variant set

The brief: one approved cover, then five on-brand derivatives — single covers, a banner crop, a merch graphic — that all read as the same visual universe. This is Kontext's home turf. Because it edits in context rather than regenerating from scratch, you can chain "same scene, new composition" requests and the palette, grain, and character survive each step. Seedream 4.5 attacks the same job differently: feed it up to 14 reference images and ask for a batch that matches, which works well when you already have several approved frames. Nano Banana is the volume play — at 1 credit per render, generating twenty candidates and culling is a legitimate strategy.

Test 4: Keyframe stills for the video handoff

The brief: a matched start-frame and end-frame — same subject, same color grade, different pose and lighting — to hand to an image-to-video model or a transition tool. This is the most underrated image job in a musician's pipeline, because keyframe quality caps video quality. Kontext's local editing is ideal for deriving an end-frame from a start-frame ("same room, same subject, now at the window, golden hour"). Nano Banana Pro's scene relighting — Google demonstrates day-to-night transforms — covers the classic verse-to-chorus mood flip. Once you have the pair, Seedance's video tools accept them directly as start/end frames and @image references (up to 9 images per render), which is how you get character consistency on the video side. The honest summary: there is no overall winner, and anyone declaring one is selling something. Nano Banana wins the jobs musicians do most often (edits, iteration volume), SeeDream 4.5 wins finished photoreal masters, and Kontext wins anything that must stay visually consistent across a set. Flux Krea is the wildcard when the brief is pure from-scratch generation with an anti-AI aesthetic.

Frequently asked questions

Which AI image engine is best for album covers with readable text?+

Nano Banana Pro is the current leader for typography on artwork — Google built it to render legible, multi-language text directly in images at 2K and 4K. Seedream 4.5 is a strong second, with specifically improved small-text rendering over version 4.0. Flux Kontext handles text acceptably but it is not the model's headline strength.

Is Nano Banana the same thing as Gemini's image model?+

Yes — Nano Banana is Google's name for the image generation and editing models in the Gemini family. The Pro version is built on Gemini 3 Pro and adds 2K/4K output, stronger text rendering, blending of up to 14 input images, and consistency for up to 5 people. Inside Seedance, the base engine costs 1 credit per render with a Pro mode toggle for higher resolutions.

Which engine should I use for start and end keyframes in AI music videos?+

Flux Kontext is the safest pick because it edits in context: you can derive an end-frame from your start-frame while keeping the subject, palette, and grain identical. Nano Banana Pro is best when the frames differ mainly in lighting, since it can relight a scene (for example day to night). Feed the finished pair into an image-to-video or transition tool as start/end frames.

Can I use images from these engines commercially as a musician?+

The free tier's 1 credit per 24 hours is watermarked and does not include commercial rights, so treat it as a test lane only.
