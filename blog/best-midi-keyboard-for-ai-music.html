The Best MIDI Keyboard for Beginners Making AI Music (What the Specs and Reviewers Say)

By the AI Music Video GuideUpdated November 8, 20269 min read

Quick answer: if you mostly generate songs with AI tools and want to play a melody or a bass line back in, a 25-key mini controller with pads is enough and sits beside a laptop. If you want to learn chords with two hands or re-record a piano part, buy 49 full-size keys. Every pick here runs on USB bus power.

Do you need 25 or 49 keys to play along with AI-generated music?

Twenty-five keys cover two octaves, enough for a bass line, a lead or a one-handed chord pad, and the octave buttons move the range. Forty-nine keys give four octaves, so you can hold a chord in the left hand and play a melody in the right without shifting. Pick 49 if you will ever re-record a piano part.

Say a generator produced a song you like, you pulled the stems into a DAW, and now a hook needs fixing. A MIDI keyboard sends note data, not sound, so you can replay a wobbly AI lead on a clean synth or layer real chords under a generated vocal. Our guide to AI music generators covers which tools export stems.

Mini keys or full-size keys for a beginner?

Mini keys save space and weight, but reviewers keep describing them as cramped, and two of the reviews we read say serious players will want a better keyboard. Full-size synth-action keys are easier to learn fingering on and closer to a piano. If you have never played, we would choose mini keys only when the desk or the bag forces it.

The complaints repeat across brands: MusicRadar says the newest Akai's mini keys can feel pretty cramped, and Sound On Sound wrote that the 32-key Native Instruments board's mini keys probably won't appeal to serious players. Full-size does not mean weighted, either: of the 49-key boards only the Launchkey 49 MK4 lists semi-weighted keys. So the best midi keyboard for beginners who want to learn properly is a full-size synth-action board, not a piano substitute.

Do pads and knobs matter if the AI already made the beat?

Yes, more than you would expect. Pads let you tap a fill or trigger a sample in time over a generated track, and knobs let you ride a filter or a volume while the song plays, which is how an AI stem starts to feel performed rather than pasted. If you only want to play notes, buy more keys instead.

The layouts split into two camps. The MiniLab 3 has x8 encoders, x4 faders and x8 pads, the Launchkey Mini 25 MK4 has 16 pads with polyphonic aftertouch and 8 endless encoders, and the Alesis V49 MKII pairs 8 LED-backlit pads with 4 assignable knobs with full-size keys. The other camp is keys plus a few buttons: both Korgs, the Keystation 49 MK3 and the Nektar SE49.

Entry tier: 25 mini keys with pads

For most people reading this, the best midi keyboard for beginners is in this tier: two octaves of mini keys, pads, knobs, and a bundle that means you do not need to buy a DAW on day one.

We did not lab-test these products ourselves; this guide combines maker specifications with what independent reviewers report, with sources linked. As an Amazon Associate I earn from qualifying purchases.

Arturia MiniLab 3 (paid link)

Arturia lists 25 slim keys, 8 encoders, 4 faders, 8 pads, a mini display, a pedal input and a 5-pin MIDI out, and the store page gives 355 x 220 x 50 mm at 1.5 kg. The bundle is Analog Lab Intro, Ableton Live Lite, Melodics, UVI Model D, Loopcloud and a Native Instruments licence. Sound On Sound called it a compelling starter package and portable controller.

The weaknesses come from the same review: the cut-down synth library has around 500 patches against 9000 in the full version, and because the faders are mapped to EQ, only four macros per patch actually control the instrument.

Akai MPK Mini MK3 (paid link)

MusicRadar lists 25 velocity sensitive keys, 8 velocity and pressure sensitive MPC pads with aftertouch, 8 continuous rotary knobs, a thumb stick for pitchbend and modulation and a sustain pedal input, and Akai's FAQ gives 1.65 lbs. The review names Hybrid 3, Mini Grand and Velvet plus MPC Beats in the bundle; read the current listing, because it has changed over the years.

The pads are the draw; the joystick is the knock, since it still requires some skill to elicit just modulation. Akai's own site now lists only the fourth version, the MPK Mini IV, which replaces the joystick with wheels and adds a 5-pin MIDI output, a USB-C port and a weight of 1.05 kg; the MK3 is the previous model, still widely sold, and MusicRadar calls it still a very valid MIDI controller.

Novation Launchkey Mini 25 MK4 (paid link)

Novation lists 25 synth-action mini keys, 16 RGB pads with polyphonic aftertouch, 8 endless encoders, a 128 x 64 OLED, a USB-C socket, a 3.5mm MIDI out and a sustain jack, at 338.5 by 176.8 by 49 mm and 0.78 kg. The bundle is Ableton Live Lite, Cubase LE, Novation Play, Komplete 15 Select and Melodics, and Scale Mode locks the keys to one of 30 scales.

Sound On Sound's downsides: the screen is very small, the documentation is thin, and there is no off button or sleep mode. The keys have no aftertouch; only the pads do.

Korg nanoKEY2 (paid link)

This is the one for a laptop bag. Korg lists a 25-key velocity keyboard with pitch, octave, mod and sustain buttons, a mini-USB port, 325 x 83 x 16 mm and 244 g, with no pads or knobs. MusicRadar says the action is light and the unit is still a little awkward: sketch on it, do not finger-drum on it.

Middle tier: more keys, still portable

Native Instruments Komplete Kontrol M32 (paid link)

Native Instruments lists 32 compact synth-action keys, eight touch-sensitive knobs, two touch strips, an OLED display and a TRS pedal input, and MusicRadar gives 457 x 167 x 50 mm and 1.45 kg. Smart Play offers over 100 scales and modes, and registration brings Komplete 15 Select plus Ableton Live Lite. Sound On Sound's cons: limited visual feedback, USB only and a fixed MIDI template.

Korg microKEY2 (paid link)

Korg sells it in 25, 37, 49 and 61-key versions; the 37, 49 and 61 get pitch and mod wheels and a damper pedal jack. The 49 measures 709 x 139 x 54 mm and weighs 1.4 kg, and the software code covers iZotope Ozone Elements, Skoove and Korg synths. No pads, knobs or display.

Full-size tier: 49 keys for two-handed playing

Novation Launchkey 49 MK4 (paid link)

Novation lists 49 semi-weighted piano-style keys, 16 RGB pads, 8 endless encoders, 9 faders, a USB-C socket, a 5-pin MIDI out and a sustain jack, at 730 by 263.5 by 93 mm and 4.08 kg.

Novation says it can split the keyboard into two zones or layer two MIDI channels, which Sound On Sound notes the smaller ones do not, and its bundle lists the same Ableton Live Lite, Cubase LE, Komplete 15 Select and Melodics. Sound On Sound's one knock: the 49 and 61 are a bit on the chunky side.

Alesis V49 MKII (paid link)

Alesis lists 49 full-size synth-action keys, 8 LED-backlit pads with full level and note repeat, 4 assignable knobs, an arpeggiator with 6 modes and a sustain input, with MPC Beats, 7 expansion packs and a 30-day Melodics trial. MusicRadar calls the keys full-size, firmly-sprung, synth-action.

M-Audio Keystation 49 MK3 (paid link)

M-Audio lists 49 full-size velocity-sensitive synth-action keys, a volume fader, transport and directional buttons, pitch and modulation wheels and a sustain pedal input, and says it is USB-powered, class-compliant and works with an iPad through the Apple camera adapter.

The bundle on the current page is Ableton Live Lite, MPC Beats, Mini Grand, Velvet, Xpand!2, TubeSynth, Electric, Bassline, Skoove, Melodics and 2 GB of Touch Loops; the 2018 launch list included Pro Tools First, which the current page no longer names, so check the current listing before you count on one plug-in. M-Audio does not list a weight.

Nektar SE49 (paid link)

Nektar lists 49 velocity-sensitive full-size synth-action keys, 4 velocity curves plus 3 fixed ones, four buttons, a 30 mm fader and a footswitch input, at 80.5 x 19.7 x 6.9 cm and 2.2 kg.

It ships with Bitwig 8-Track, with over 50 instruments and effects on up to 8 tracks, and Nektar's integration adds transport control in Bitwig, Cubase, GarageBand, Logic and Reaper. MusicRadar's note on the 25-key SE25 sibling: you need the manual for the deep operations.

Will these work with an iPad or a USB-C laptop?

All of them run on USB bus power with no separate supply, and the makers that address drivers (Arturia, Akai, Korg and M-Audio) say none is needed on a Mac or Windows laptop, but only a few have a USB-C port on the keyboard itself. iPad use is uneven: some makers list it, some need a powered hub or an adapter, and one reviewer found the 32-key model draws more power than an iPad supplies.

The MiniLab 3 store page says USB-C connects to computers and iPad, and the Launchkey Mini 25 MK4 and the Launchkey 49 MK4 both list a USB-C socket. The MPK Mini MK3 uses a standard USB-A to USB-B cable and Akai says it does not have a low power mode to operate with an iOS device. Sound On Sound found the M32 takes more power than an iPad can provide.

Which one should you buy?

  • Beats over AI stems on a laptop: the Launchkey Mini 25 MK4, or the MiniLab 3 if you want faders and a MIDI out.
  • The smallest thing that plays notes: the nanoKEY2; range without width: the microKEY2 49.
  • Learning two-handed chords: the Launchkey 49 MK4 with pads and faders, or the Alesis V49 MKII or Nektar SE49 for keys and little else.

The bundled Lite DAW is enough to start, and a sustain pedal is the first add-on for a 49-key board. The rest of our home-setup picks are on the studio gear list.

Frequently asked questions

Can I plug a MIDI keyboard straight into an AI music generator?

Usually not. Most generators work from a text prompt and give you audio, so the keyboard comes in afterwards: export the song or its stems, open them in a DAW, and play new parts on top or replace a line you dislike. The keyboard's job is the overdub, not the prompt.

Do I need to read music or know theory to use one?

No. Several of the controllers here have a scale mode that mutes or corrects wrong notes and a chord mode that plays a full chord from one key, and the bundled lesson apps teach the basics. You will still learn faster if you look up the key of the AI track first and set the controller to match it.

What else do I need besides the keyboard?

A computer with a DAW, which the bundled Lite versions cover, the USB cable that comes in the box, and headphones. You do not need an audio interface, because a MIDI controller sends note data rather than sound. A sustain pedal is worth adding, and every board here except the nanoKEY2 has a pedal input; the nanoKEY2 uses a sustain button instead.

Will a small starter controller hold me back later?

The keys are the part you outgrow. Pads, knobs and software bundles are similar across this whole group, but mini keys and light synth action start to frustrate once you can play. Buying 49 full-size keys first skips that step; buying a mini board first takes less desk space and still works as a second controller for pads later.

Is a 61-key or fully weighted keyboard better for a beginner?

Only if piano is the goal. Weighted boards are heavier and deeper, and 61 keys rarely fit beside a laptop. For someone who wants to play parts into a DAW over AI-generated tracks, 49 synth-action keys are the usual compromise, and a 25-key board is fine for sketching.
