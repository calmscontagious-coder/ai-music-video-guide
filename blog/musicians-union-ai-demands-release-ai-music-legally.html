Musicians Union AI Demands: Can You Still Release AI-Assisted Music?

By the AI Music Video GuideUpdated October 21, 202612 min read

how this page earns

Quick answer: Yes. As of October 21, 2026, nothing in the musicians union's new demands stops an independent artist from releasing AI-assisted music. The AFM campaign targets a contract between the union and four major labels. Your release still turns on three older rules: the tool's terms, each platform's AI policy, and honest disclosure at upload.

We release AI-assisted tracks and we pay for the tools that make them, so the musicians union AI demands landed on our desk as a question about whether the next upload is still allowed. We read the campaign material, the filings, the tool terms and the platform policy pages. Every date below comes from a page we opened on October 21, 2026, or carries its own 2026 publication date.

What is the musicians union demanding?

The American Federation of Musicians launched a campaign called Human Made, Human Paid on Tuesday, October 20, 2026. It wants three things written into its Sound Recording Labor Agreement with Universal, Sony, Warner and Hollywood Records: consent before a member's recording is used to train AI, credit when it is, and payment for both AI use and the licensing deals the labels sign (Rolling Stone, October 20, 2026).

The AFM covers more than 80,000 professional musicians. The contract in question, the SRLA, sets the terms under which session players are hired for label recordings. Talks on the next version restart in November 2026, after rounds in January and March (AFM rally page, March 2026).

The campaign has a lawsuit underneath it. On June 5, 2026, the union sued Universal and Warner in New York, arguing that licensing member recordings to Suno and Udio was a new use under Article 21(a) of the SRLA, which would owe the musicians a payment (Music Business Worldwide, June 2026). Briefing on the labels' motion to dismiss closed September 11, 2026, with Universal arguing the union had conceded that no AFM agreement covers AI use, so there is no rate to pay (Music Business Worldwide, September 2026).

That concession is why the campaign exists. A judge can only enforce what the contract says. Bargaining writes what it doesn't say yet.

Does the AFM campaign change what an independent artist can release?

Not directly, and not this year. The SRLA binds the union and four labels, not you, your distributor or any streaming service. Unless you're a union session player hired for a major-label recording, no clause in that contract reaches your next upload.

What it could change, months from now, is what the licensed models are allowed to learn from and how their output gets labeled. Two indirect effects are worth planning for. A consent clause would mean some recordings need a yes before they enter a training set, so licensed models could learn from less or ship slower. A credit clause means someone keeps a list of which recordings went in, and lists like that tend to feed detection and tagging systems (Mubert, October 2026).

Is a licensed model safer to release from than an unlicensed one?

Safer in one narrow way and not at all in another. A licensed model lowers the chance that a rights holder comes after your output, because the labels that licensed the training agreed to it. It does nothing for platform policy: Deezer's detector, Tidal's no-pay rule and Bandcamp's ban act on what the audio is, not on what the model learned from (Stability AI explainer, September 25, 2026).

Licensed also means licensed by some, not by all. Suno released v6 on September 9, 2026, built in partnership with Warner, BMG and Believe. Nine days later, on September 18, 2026, Universal and Sony filed a second suit against Suno in Massachusetts over 60,202 recordings, arguing that v6 was trained to reproduce the behavior of the older models. Their complaint says "v6 is not a fresh start; it is the fruit of the same poisoned tree" (Digital Music News, September 18, 2026). A model licensed by one major is still being sued by the other two.

Udio went the other way. It settled with Universal on October 29, 2025, and disabled downloads of audio, video and stems the same day (Udio Help Center, checked October 21, 2026). That is still the case in October 2026. You can generate and share a link, but you can't release a Udio song anywhere.

What do Suno's September 2026 terms mean for a release?

Since September 3, 2026, the download is the licence. A song you generated on Pro or Premier and downloaded while subscribed carries commercial rights. A song you never downloaded doesn't, and free-tier trial downloads are for personal use only (Suno Help Center FAQ, checked October 21, 2026).

Pro gets 20 downloads a month and Premier gets 60, resetting on your billing date with no rollover. Premier subscribers inside Suno Studio have no cap. Free accounts opened before September 3 got up to 7 lifetime trial downloads (Suno blog, August 10, 2026). One song is one download whatever the format, stems included, and re-downloading is free.

Prices, as checked against suno.com by Undetectr on September 25, 2026: Pro is $10 a month, Premier is $30, and extra downloads cost $2.99 each. Rights on a download you've taken survive cancellation (Undetectr, September 25, 2026). Suno's terms also promise no copyright in an output. You own the file under contract, which isn't the same as owning a protected composition.

We find the cap annoying and the logic clean. One release costs one download. Download everything you intend to sell before you cancel, because an undownloaded song on a lapsed plan reverts to free-tier rules. Where can you release AI-assisted music right now?

Everywhere, with conditions; only tracks that are fully or substantially AI-generated are barred, on Bandcamp and Qobuz. Spotify, Apple Music and YouTube accept AI-assisted music and ask you to declare it. Deezer and Tidal detect it themselves and limit reach or money for tracks they judge fully generated (Stability AI, September 25, 2026).

No platform checks what the model was trained on; every rule in the table acts on the audio itself. Deezer reported that fully AI-generated tracks passed 50 percent of daily uploads at peak in June 2026, around 90,000 tracks a day, while accounting for only 1 to 3 percent of streams, and that up to 85 percent of streams on those tracks in 2025 were fraudulent (Deezer Newsroom, July 21, 2026). Spotify said it removed more than 75 million spammy tracks in the twelve months before its September 25, 2025 AI policy (Spotify Newsroom). These rules aren't aimed at a person making twelve songs a year. They're aimed at pipelines making 90,000 a day.

Spotify's newest step is the AI Persona badge. From mid-September 2026, AI-generated artist identities carry a badge and sit outside personalized recommendations by default (Spotify Newsroom, August 11, 2026). Release under your own name and this doesn't touch you. Run a virtual artist and it changes the discovery math. How do you release through DistroKid without getting pulled?

Own 100 percent of what you upload, don't imitate anyone's voice, don't flood, and tick the AI credits honestly. DistroKid accepts music made with AI tools under those four rules (DistroKid Help Center, checked October 21, 2026). Since spring 2026 its upload form asks which parts of a track were AI-generated and passes that answer to Spotify's AI Credits (Digital Music News, May 14, 2026).

One caution for October 2026. Universal sued DistroKid in Delaware federal court on September 15, 2026, alleging it floods platforms with AI-generated tracks, and DistroKid is not among the initial participants in the majors-backed Streaming Integrity Initiative announced the day before (Digital Music News, September 15, 2026). DistroKid has since started removing releases, and some artists say fully human, sample-free tracks were pulled too (Digital Music News, October 12, 2026). In a filing reported on October 16, 2026, Universal and Sony asked a New York federal court to force DistroKid to disclose its AI upload volumes (Digital Music News, October 16, 2026). Keep your stems, lyric drafts and session files so you can appeal a removal, though some artists in that report said their appeals had gone nowhere yet.

The disclosure covers four roles: lyrics, vocals, instrumentals and production. It shows in Song Credits on Spotify's mobile app and says how AI contributed, not that the whole track is AI (Spotify Support, checked October 21, 2026). The field is self-reported, and both companies say the absence of a credit proves nothing.

We tick it every time. Disclosure costs nothing today; being found out later, when detection catches up, costs the account.

On cost: DistroKid's own plans page lists memberships from $2.08 a month, which works out to about $25 a year for one artist name with unlimited uploads (DistroKid plans page, checked October 21, 2026). Add Suno Pro at $10 a month and a year of releasing runs about $145 before a single video. Our arithmetic, not a quoted bundle.

Can you copyright an AI-assisted song?

Only the parts a human authored. The US Copyright Office's Part 2 report of January 29, 2025 holds that prompts alone don't make you the author, while lyrics you wrote, a vocal you performed, or a creative selection and arrangement of generated parts can be registered with the AI material disclaimed (US Copyright Office).

The courts agree. The D.C. Circuit confirmed on March 18, 2025, in Thaler v. Perlmutter, that human authorship is required, and the Supreme Court declined to review it in March 2026 (EMVN Law Hub, 2026). The filing mechanics matter more than the theory. Use the Standard Application, not the Single one, because only it has the Limitation of Claim fields. Describe what you made under Author Created and disclaim the generated instrumental or composition under Material Excluded. Hiding the AI part can cost you the whole registration: the Office can cancel on its own, and an opponent can invoke Section 411(b) to have it disregarded (EMVN Law Hub, 2026).

This is where the union's fight and yours diverge. Session musicians have clear human authorship and are arguing about pay. A prompt-only track has no human author, so there is nothing to register. The more of the song you write, sing or arrange, the more of it is yours in law.

What about the music video?

The video carries its own disclosure duty and nothing else from this fight. If the visuals come from a video model such as Seedance, the music's rights are unchanged. What you add is YouTube's AI-use setting for realistic scenes, and Apple Music's Music Video tag if you deliver a video with the release.

YouTube requires disclosure when AI makes a real person appear to do something they didn't, alters real footage, or generates a realistic scene that didn't happen. Stylized or animated content and minor edits are exempt (YouTube Help, checked October 21, 2026). If your video shows realistic people or scenes, tick the setting; stylized or animated work doesn't need it. Apple's four Transparency Tags cover artwork, track, composition and music video, applied by labels and distributors at delivery (Music Business Worldwide, March 4, 2026).

We use Seedance for the picture once the song is cleared. It's good at the frame and useless for the rights question: it doesn't clear music, doesn't claim to, and its reference-audio feature only syncs motion to a file you already have the right to use. Clear the track, then make the video. Our YouTube monetization guide for AI music videos covers the disclosure checkbox and Partner Program rules.

What should you do before the November talks?

Download every track you plan to release while your paid plan is active, disclose the AI parts at upload, register the human parts, and keep your process files. None of these steps depends on how the SRLA talks end, and each one helps if a distributor questions a release.

  1. List the songs you intend to sell. On Suno, download each while subscribed. One download per song, stems included; the right survives cancellation.
  2. At upload, answer the AI credits question role by role. If the lyrics are yours and the instrumental isn't, say so.
  3. Release under a real artist identity if you want Spotify recommendations. AI Persona profiles are badged and excluded from algorithmic placement from mid-September 2026.
  4. Skip Bandcamp and Qobuz for fully generated tracks (Bandcamp also bars substantially generated ones) and expect no Tidal royalties on them.
  5. Register what you wrote: Standard Application, human parts claimed, AI parts disclaimed, drafts saved with the deposit copy.
  6. Watch the SRLA talks in November 2026 and the ruling on the labels' motion to dismiss, fully briefed since September 11, 2026. Either could change what licensed models learn from in 2027. Neither changes a track you've already downloaded and disclosed.

Frequently asked questions

Can I still legally release a song made with Suno after the AFM campaign?+

Yes. The union's demands target the Sound Recording Labor Agreement between the AFM and four major labels, and that contract doesn't bind independent artists, distributors or streaming services. Your release depends on Suno's terms, which since September 3, 2026 grant commercial rights to songs you downloaded on Pro or Premier, plus each platform's AI policy and honest disclosure at upload.

Does releasing from a licensed model like Suno v6 protect me from takedowns?+

Partly. A licensed model lowers the risk that a participating label claims the output, but Universal and Sony sued Suno again on September 18, 2026, so v6 isn't licensed by everyone. Licensing also has no effect on platform policy: Deezer's detector, Tidal's no-royalty rule and Bandcamp's ban act on what the audio is, not on what the model was trained on.

Do I have to disclose AI on DistroKid, and what happens if I don't?+

DistroKid asks which parts of a new upload were AI-generated and passes that to Spotify's AI Credits. Nobody verifies the answer today. Since Universal sued DistroKid in September 2026, the distributor has started removing tracks, and some artists say fully human releases were pulled too. Disclose honestly, own 100 percent of the rights, and keep stems and session files for an appeal.

Can I copyright a song I made with AI tools?+

Only the human-authored parts. Under the US Copyright Office's January 2025 report, prompts alone don't make you the author, but original lyrics, a recorded vocal, or a creative arrangement of generated parts can be registered with the AI material disclaimed. File the Standard Application, describe your contribution, exclude the generated elements, and keep drafts and prompt histories as evidence.

Will the November 2026 SRLA talks change my releases next year?+

Not for tracks you've already downloaded and disclosed. If the union wins consent and credit clauses, licensed models could learn from fewer major-label recordings and training-set lists might feed detection systems, which would affect future generations and labeling. The ruling on the labels' motion to dismiss the union's lawsuit, fully briefed since September 11, 2026, is the other date to watch.
