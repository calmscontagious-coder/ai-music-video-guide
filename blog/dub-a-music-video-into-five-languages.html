Dub a Music Video Into Five Languages: Dub the Talking, Caption the Singing

By the AI Music Video GuideUpdated November 13, 202610 min read

Quick answer: You can dub a music video into other languages, but we'd dub only the talking. The dubbing pages never mention lyrics or singing, so dub the spoken intro and interview, caption the lyrics, and upload each dub as an extra audio track. Creator includes 9 Dubbing v2 minutes at $22 a month (ElevenLabs dubbing studio page, read November 13, 2026).

A music video carries two kinds of audio: the song, which you want left alone, and the talking around it, such as a spoken intro, an interview cut, a studio diary or a trailer voiceover. AI dubbing is built for the second kind. This guide covers what the dubbing pages promise, what they are silent on, the minutes each plan includes, and the upload steps on the platform most musicians publish to. Every figure comes from a page we read on November 13, 2026.

We are an independent affiliate of ElevenLabs and may receive compensation if you sign up through our links. The plans that include dubbing minutes are listed on the ElevenLabs plans page through our link, and the table further down shows what each one includes.

Can AI dubbing translate the singing in a music video?

We have no vendor statement that it can. The dubbing studio page, the dubbing docs and the pricing page we read say nothing about lyrics, singing or vocals; they describe translating speech and cloning the speaker's voice. So we recommend dubbing only the spoken segments and captioning the lyrics in each target language.

The product page's promise is specific to speakers: 'Every dub delivered in a voice clone of the original speaker while maintaining voice identity, pitch, and tonality' (ElevenLabs dubbing studio page, read November 13, 2026). Speaker, not singer. The docs add that 'The original background audio is retained' and tell users to 'Avoid re-mixing music, effects, or ambient sounds' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026).

What to dub, and what to caption

  • Dub: the spoken intro, an interview segment, behind-the-scenes narration, a trailer voiceover.
  • Caption: the lyrics, in each target language, as a subtitle file or burned-in text.
  • Leave alone: the song itself. The docs say the background audio is kept, so the music bed should pass through untouched.

What is the difference between automatic dubbing and the legacy studio?

Automatic Dubbing runs on the newer Dubbing v2 model and handles translation, cloning, dubbing and sync by itself. Dubbing Studio is the hands-on editor, but it only runs the v1 model, is in maintenance mode, and caps files at 45 minutes (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). For a music video, automatic is the default.

The docs say Dubbing 'translates audio and video across 90+ languages' while keeping each speaker's voice characteristics, and that Automatic Dubbing runs on 'the latest Dubbing v2 Alpha model' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). Alpha is the vendor's word, so expect the model to change under you.

Speaker handling is where it earns its place on a video with an interview. The docs say it can 'Automatically detect multiple speakers, even with overlapping speech', 'up to 32 unique speakers per file' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). A band of four talking over each other fits inside that.

Dubbing Studio is the older route. The docs describe 'Granular control over your dubs, including transcript editing, speaker reassignment, and per-clip regeneration', then say it is 'Only available via the v1 model' and 'in maintenance mode and receives critical bug fixes only' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). Use it to fix a mistranslated line by hand; don't build a new workflow on it.

File limits differ. Automatic Dubbing takes 'Up to 1 GB and 180 minutes in the app, or 3 GB per source file via the API'; Dubbing Studio takes 'Up to 1 GB and 45 minutes' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). A music video is nowhere near either cap, so the limit that matters is dubbing minutes, not file size.

Two more docs facts: 'All self-serve plans (Free, Starter, Creator, Pro, Scale, Business) allow up to 3 concurrent dubbing jobs', and 'transcript editing and audio regeneration via the API are available on Enterprise plans only' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). If each language runs as its own job, which the docs do not state, that limit means batches of three.

What does it cost to dub a four-minute video into five languages?

Five target languages of a four-minute video is 20 dubbed minutes, our arithmetic. Billed entirely at Creator's $2.45 extra-minute rate that is $49.00; at the API's $2.20 per minute for Dubbing v2 it is $44.00, both our arithmetic from the vendor's rates (ElevenLabs dubbing studio and API pricing pages, read November 13, 2026).

Those are ceilings, not invoices. Creator includes 9 Dubbing v2 minutes a month, so in a month where you haven't used them, 9 of the 20 minutes come out of the allowance and only the remainder is billed per minute (ElevenLabs dubbing studio page, read November 13, 2026). On Starter the shape is harsher: 2 included minutes, then $2.70 per extra minute, our reading of the same table.

The API route prices Dubbing v2 at $2.20 per minute, and Dubbing v1 at $0.33 per minute with a watermark or $0.50 without (ElevenLabs API pricing page, read November 13, 2026). The dubbing studio FAQ says 'Yes, API access is available to enterprise and self-serve customers' (ElevenLabs dubbing studio page, read November 13, 2026), so the API price is not enterprise-only.

How do you get the dubbed audio back onto the video?

Download the dubbed audio and swap it in. The docs say for production you should 'download the dubbed audio and use it to replace the audio track on your original video', and that audio comes back 'mono for Dubbing v1 and at most stereo for Dubbing v2' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). Keep the music bed untouched.

For the picture, the docs say video is 'most commonly output at 1080p' (ElevenLabs dubbing docs, read November 13, 2026). The cleaner route for a music video is to ignore the video export, take the dubbed audio only, and lay it under your own master export in your editor. That keeps your grade and resolution exactly as you delivered them.

How do you publish the dubs as extra audio tracks, and when is an AI label needed?

Upload each dub as a separate language track so viewers hear their preferred language. In YouTube Studio, open Languages, pick the video, click 'Add Language', then Add next to 'Dub', choose the audio file and publish. This needs access to Advanced features, and the file must be audio only (YouTube Help multi-language audio page, read November 13, 2026).

The help page says creators can 'upload additional audio tracks in multiple languages for both new and previously uploaded videos', and that viewers hear the track matching their preferred language, which the platform works out from watch history (YouTube Help multi-language audio page, read November 13, 2026). It also says 'Multi-language audio is currently available to creators with access to Advanced features.'

Steps on a computer

  • Sign in to YouTube Studio and select Languages in the left menu.
  • Click the video, click 'Add Language' and select the language.
  • Next to 'Dub', click Add, then Select file and choose the audio file.
  • Click Publish. To replace a track later, delete the current audio for that language under 'Audio', then upload the new one (YouTube Help multi-language audio page, read November 13, 2026).

Three rules from the same page. 'This feature does not automatically generate dubbed audio tracks.' If an auto dub already exists for a language, delete it before uploading your own. And 'Files must be in a supported audio-only file format and roughly the same length as your video' (YouTube Help multi-language audio page, read November 13, 2026). No numeric size or track-count limits are listed.

One warning matters for music: if copyrighted content in the secondary track differs from the original audio, 'the file may be removed' (YouTube Help multi-language audio page, read November 13, 2026). Keeping the same music bed under the dubbed speech is what the dubbing docs describe, and it avoids the difference the help page warns about, but neither page promises the check will pass, so listen to each dub against the original before publishing.

Why not use the platform's own automatic dubbing?

'Automatic dubbing generates translated audio tracks in different languages', and it is on by default for eligible creators (YouTube Help automatic dubbing page, read November 13, 2026). But a video may be ineligible if it 'exceeds 120 minutes', has little or no speech, or contains copyrighted content, and 'You cannot edit automatic dubs' (YouTube Help automatic dubbing page, read November 13, 2026). A music video that is mostly singing likely falls under the little-or-no-speech rule, and the copyrighted-content rule may also apply if it includes other people's material.

The AI label

The disclosure rule has an explicit carve-out: no disclosure is needed for 'Cloning one's own voice to create voice overs or dubs' (YouTube Help AI disclosure page, read November 13, 2026). A dub of your own spoken intro in your own cloned voice is on the no-label list, alongside 'Caption creation'. The carve-out on that page is for cloning your own voice. It does not mention dubbing someone else's voice in an interview, so check the page's real-person rule for that case.

Disclosure is required for realistic AI content that 'Makes a real person appear to say or do something they didn't do' or 'Creates music that's the main focus of the video' (YouTube Help AI disclosure page, read November 13, 2026). If the song itself is AI-generated, that second trigger applies regardless of the dub. You set it during upload under Attributes, 'AI use', Yes or No, and the platform may also apply labels automatically (YouTube Help AI disclosure page, read November 13, 2026).

Other platforms have their own rules; our guide to AI music video rules on TikTok, Spotify and Instagram covers those.

What we'd still like to know and couldn't find on any page we read: whether sung lines are translated, skipped or mangled, and how a partial minute is billed. Until a vendor page says, dub the talking, caption the singing, and test one language before paying for five.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI dubbing translate the lyrics of my song?

We have no vendor statement that it will. The dubbing pages we read describe translating speech and cloning the speaker's voice, keeping the background audio, and say nothing about lyrics or singing. Our recommendation is to dub only the spoken parts of the video and to caption the lyrics in each target language, then test one language before paying for more.

How many dubbing minutes does a plan include?

On the AI voice generator we link, the dubbing page lists Dubbing v2 minutes per plan: 0.4 on Free, 2 on Starter at $6 a month, 9 on Creator at $22 and 44 on Pro at $99. Extra minutes cost $4.91, $2.70, $2.45 and $2.23 in that order, excluding taxes. Those figures were read on November 13, 2026 and can change.

What does it cost to dub a four-minute video into five languages?

Five languages of a four-minute video is 20 dubbed minutes, our arithmetic. Billed entirely at the Creator plan's $2.45 extra-minute rate that is $49.00, and at the API's $2.20 per minute it is $44.00, both our arithmetic from the vendor's published rates. Creator includes 9 minutes a month, so the real bill is lower in a month where the allowance is unused.

How do I add a dubbed track to a video I already uploaded?

The video platform's help page says additional audio tracks can be added to both new and previously uploaded videos, if your channel has access to Advanced features. On a computer, open Languages in the studio's left menu, click the video, click 'Add Language', click Add next to 'Dub', select the audio file and publish. The file must be audio only and roughly the same length as the video.

Do I need an AI label on a dubbed music video?

Not for the dub itself if it is your own cloned voice: the platform's disclosure page lists that among the things needing no disclosure. A label is required for realistic AI content that makes a real person appear to say something they didn't, or that creates music that is the main focus of the video, which can apply to the song regardless of the dub.
